# Swahili Register, Greetings, and Address

**Purpose**: Set the right level of respect and warmth in Kiswahili copy. East African audiences read register through greetings and forms of address; getting these right signals a native, respectful brand.

**Read this when**: You are choosing the brand voice, writing openings and welcomes, addressing customers, or deciding between standard Swahili and a casual voice.

## Default register: Kiswahili sanifu

Write standard Swahili (`Kiswahili sanifu`) — the form based on the Zanzibar `Kiunguja` dialect, taught in schools and used in media across Tanzania and Kenya. It is universally understood and reads as professional. Keep copy in `sanifu` unless the brief explicitly asks for a youth-slang voice.

- Avoid `Sheng` (the Nairobi Swahili-English slang) and other street registers in standard copy; they date fast, exclude older readers, and read as unprofessional to many.
- Avoid heavy coastal-dialect forms (`Kimvita`, `Kiamu`) in pan-regional copy; they read as local colour, not standard.
- Tanzania carries Swahili as the deeper national language with the widest formal vocabulary; Kenyan audiences code-switch with English more readily. Decide the market and hold its conventions.

## Greetings by register

Greetings are not decoration — they set the social relationship and are expected. Match the register to the brand and audience.

- Neutral / universal: `Habari?` / `Habari gani?` (how are things?) → reply `Nzuri` / `Njema` / `Salama`. `Habari za asubuhi/mchana/jioni` (good morning/afternoon/evening).
- Warm and personal: `Hujambo?` (sg) / `Hamjambo?` (pl) → `Sijambo` / `Hatujambo`.
- Respectful, to an elder or someone senior: `Shikamoo` → answered `Marahaba`. Use when the brand deliberately defers to the reader's seniority; `Shikamoo` from a brand to all readers can feel overdone — reserve it for contexts that warrant deference.
- Hospitality: `Karibu` (sg) / `Karibuni` (pl) — welcome; `Karibu tena` — welcome back, good for returning customers and loyalty messaging. `Karibu sana` — a warmer welcome.
- Entry framing: `Hodi?` (may I come in?) → `Karibu` (come in / welcome). A culturally resonant way to frame "start here" or "step inside".

A natural page opener: `Karibu! Tunafurahi kukuona hapa.` (Welcome! We are glad to see you here.)

## Honorifics and address

- `Bwana` (Mr / Sir), `Bibi` / `Bi.` (Mrs / Ms / Madam — `Bi.` before a name), `Mama` and `Baba` as respectful address for adult women and men in many contexts.
- `Mzee` (respected elder) — dignity, used for senior figures.
- `Mwalimu` (teacher) — respect for an expert or authority, not only literal teachers.
- `Ndugu` (brother/sister, comrade) — solidarity and equality; warm without being junior.
- `Mheshimiwa` (honourable) — formal, for officials; rarely needed in commerce.
- When the reader's gender is unknown, prefer relationship-neutral framing (`ndugu`, `mteja wetu` — "our customer") over guessing `Bwana`/`Bibi`.

## Singular vs plural address, and plural-of-respect

- Address one reader with the singular `u-`/`-ako` forms for a personal, direct voice: `Tunakusaidia` (we help you), `oda yako` (your order).
- Use the plural `m-`/`-enu` forms when addressing the audience as a group, or to show added respect to one person: `Karibuni`, `Tunawakaribisha` (we welcome you all). Pick one stance per page and hold it.
- Swahili politeness leans on warmth and the right honorific more than on a `tu`/`vous`-style grammatical split; consistency and respectful word choice carry the register.

## Brand voice ("we")

- The company speaks as `sisi` (we), carried by the `tu-` subject prefix: `Tunatoa…` (we provide), `Tumejitolea…` (we are committed), `Tutakuhudumia…` (we will serve you).
- `Tumejitolea kukuhudumia kwa ubora.` (We are committed to serving you with quality.) reads as a confident, native brand statement.

## Closings (email and messages)

- Warm-professional: `Wako mwaminifu` (yours faithfully), `Kwa heshima` (with respect), `Asante kwa muda wako` (thank you for your time).
- Friendly: `Tukutane tena` (let's meet again), `Karibu tena`.
- Blessing closings (`Mungu akubariki` — God bless you) are warm and broadly accepted in East Africa; use where the brand tone welcomes them, and keep them optional for secular or multi-faith audiences.

## Service and warmth phrases (copy-ready)

`Safari njema!` (have a good trip — warm sign-off) · `Hakuna matata` / `Hakuna tatizo` / `Bila ya wasiwasi` (no problem / no worries — reassuring) · `Hongera!` (congratulations / well done) · `Asante kwa ukarimu wako` (thank you for your hospitality) · `Ni furaha yangu` (my pleasure) · `Nakutakia mema` / `Nakutakia siku njema` (best wishes / have a good day) · `Tuko tayari kukusaidia` (we are ready to help you). Note: `Hakuna matata` is fine as a reassurance phrase in service copy, but never as your headline "Swahili flavour" aimed at locals — see the tourist-Swahili caution in `references/idiom-and-hooks.md`.

## Relationship before the transaction

In East African commerce the `duka` (shop) is a social space; jumping straight to a hard sell reads as cold. Lead with the welcome and acknowledgment, then the offer — the proverb `Mgeni njoo mwenyeji apone` ("guest, come, so the host may prosper") frames the customer as a blessing who benefits the host. Inclusive `tu-` framing (`Tujenge pamoja` — let's build together) resonates more than commands.

## Tone summary for copy

1. Open with `Karibu` or a `Habari`-family greeting where a greeting fits.
2. Address the reader consistently (singular-personal or plural-respectful) and keep honorifics appropriate.
3. Speak as `sisi`/`tu-` for the brand.
4. Stay in `Kiswahili sanifu`; keep slang and heavy dialect out of standard copy.
5. Use `asante`, `tafadhali`, and `naomba` to keep the voice courteous.

## For social / short-form

- **Open warm even in a caption.** `Karibu!` or `Habari!` as the first beat earns the read; then deliver the value. A bio: `Tunakuhudumia kwa heshima. Agiza mtandaoni, tunakuletea nyumbani.`
- **Pick singular vs plural address and hold it across posts and replies.** `Karibuni` / `Asanteni` (plural-of-respect) reads warm-and-respectful to an audience; singular `Karibu` / `Asante` for an intimate one-to-one tone.
- **Comment/DM replies stay in `Kiswahili sanifu` and stay courteous** — concede, then help: `Asante kwa ujumbe wako. Tuandikie kwa ujumbe binafsi (DM) tukurekebishie haraka.` Never slide into slang or match a hostile tone in a professional brand.
- **`Sheng` only for Kenyan youth/lifestyle briefs.** For finance, health, government, or pan-regional social, clean sanifu signals respect.
