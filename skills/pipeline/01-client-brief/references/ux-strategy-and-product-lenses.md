# Digital Product and Experience Lenses for Marketing Briefs

Read this at intake when a brief involves a new digital product, an app or portal launch, a website-led campaign, or stakeholders who disagree about what "good" looks like. It helps the strategist screen the brief, scope the engagement and hand clean requirements to `website-skills` or `design-system-skills`. It does not design or build anything. Destination-page heuristics for ads live in `advertising/ad-to-site-journey-handoff`.

## 1. Brief-screening checklist (run before accepting the brief)

A sound digital plan needs four things at the same time, not one after another: a business reason the organisation can fund, an offer that is genuinely better or cheaper for someone, evidence from real customers, and an experience people can actually use. Screen the brief with these questions; each "no" becomes an intake question, not a reason to start work.

| Screen | Ask | Red flag that means "not yet a strategy" |
|---|---|---|
| Business case | What business result will this move, by when, and who pays for it? | The brief is a single exciting idea or a list of features |
| Customer evidence | Which customers told us, by their behaviour, that they want this? | A thorough plan with no customer contact, or an assumption about "all our customers" stated as fact |
| Distinctiveness | Why would the customer choose this over what they do today? | A string of fashionable words; an expert's say-so presented as proof |
| Capability fit | Can the organisation build, run and support it? | A grand vision far beyond current people, money or systems |
| Adaptability | How will we learn and change course after launch? | A fixed destination with no feedback loop; motivational slogans in place of objectives |
| Whole experience | Does it cover every touchpoint, online and offline, not only one app or page? | "It is a branding job" — a strong brand cannot rescue a poor experience |

Separate the two jobs at kickoff: **strategy** decides what to make and why, before anything is made; **design** makes it. Write both into the brief with separate owners.

**Worked example (illustrative):** a Nairobi insurer asks for "an app that makes us look innovative". Screening turns it into: the business wants claims settled faster; policyholders already send claim photos on WhatsApp; the first experiment is a WhatsApp claim-intake flow with a two-week test, not an app.

## 2. Business model and value checks

- **Nine-part business model check:** customers, promise, channels, relationship, revenue, key resources, key activities, partners and costs (the Business Model Canvas, Osterwalder & Pigneur). Customers inform the first two; the organisation's leaders inform resources and partners.
- **Where the advantage comes from:** lowest cost, or a difference buyers value; digital offers usually compete on a better experience.
- **Better and cheaper at once:** look for a change that raises value while lowering cost; often the breakthrough is changing how customers think the task is done.
- **Improvement or disruption:** improving for existing customers is different from starting with a simpler, cheaper offer for overlooked buyers and moving upward. Name which one the brief is attempting.

## 3. Proving demand before building

1. Treat an idea as validated only when a defined group shows it values the offer through behaviour — a sign-up, deposit, pre-order or repeat use — not through opinion.
2. Build the smallest version that tests the core promise; measure; learn; change the customer group or the problem if the evidence says so.
3. If the budget has no room to test, ask for a short validation window before the full build and record the risk if it is refused.
4. Choose the cheapest experiment that answers the question:

| Question | Experiment |
|---|---|
| Will people want it? | Landing page or ad test with a sign-up or deposit |
| Will they use the service? | Deliver it by hand for a few customers before automating |
| Will the experience make sense? | A simulated front end with people doing the work behind it; or a five-screen prototype (start, two key moments, the result, the price) |
| Which moments matter most? | A storyboard of the valuable moments, online and offline, before any screen design; borrow patterns from other industries while respecting intellectual property |

5. For quick field research: set the hypothesis; write screening questions and open questions about past behaviour; choose a venue and roles (host, interviewer, note-taker); recruit with a modest incentive and consent; stagger appointments. Personal data collected must meet Uganda DPPA / Kenya DPA duties (register PL-01, PL-02).
6. Measure each stage with a question that can be answered with a number (see the funnel matrix in `strategy-customer-value-journey`).

## 4. When stakeholders disagree about "good design"

Name the disagreement before resolving it:

| Lens | Values | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| Task and productivity | Speed, fewer errors | Can people complete the job without mistakes? |
| Human limits | Fit to attention and memory | Is the page within what people can hold in mind? |
| Meaning and context | Emotion, social setting, the minority case | Does it feel right for this audience in this situation, including the people who do not fit the average? |

Also check three perspectives — how the product fits the person's life, how the person operates it, and how it makes them feel. Visual appeal works as a first filter (a cluttered page is abandoned before its content is judged), but the right look depends on the task: playful suits a promotion, not a loan application.

## 5. Scope, outcomes and minimum interface requirements

- **Declare the scope level:** one interaction, a journey across channels, or the whole customer relationship.
- **Usability qualities to require:** easy to learn, quick once learned, easy to return to, errors prevented and handled politely, satisfying to use.
- **Five outcomes any client-facing experience must meet:** useful, easy, efficient, pleasing, accessible (target per `website-skills`; register CW-05 names WCAG 2.2 AA as the current W3C Recommendation, checked 2026-09-23).
- **Interface requirements to write into the brief:** consistent patterns; confirmations, loading states and a way back; everyday words; a contact route on every page; nothing that does not serve the task; clear content and visual hierarchy; information in digestible pieces; decisions backed by data; expert review and user testing before launch.
- **Review list:** is status always visible; does the language match the user's world; can users undo and go back; is it consistent; are errors prevented and explained; can users recognise rather than remember; are there shortcuts for regulars; is it minimal; is help available?

## 6. Running and resourcing UX work

- **Set client expectations:** design is how it works, not a skin; the interface must explain itself; it takes several iterations; the client's decision-maker must take part and reply quickly; it must be tested; it continues after launch.
- **Process by stakes:** high-value surfaces get prototypes and user tests before visual design; simpler surfaces go from sketch to wireframe to visual; low-visibility admin screens can go straight from sketch to build. A typical sequence: audience and analytics → journeys → wireframes and prototypes → tests and iteration → look-and-feel options → detailed design → A/B tests of key copy.
- **Collaboration:** a kickoff workshop with everyone who touches the product; a short weekly design meeting with one person per function; clickable wireframes instead of long specifications; user evidence settles disputes.
- **Roles:** screen design, flow and structure design, and user research are different jobs; one person doing all three usually drops research.
- **Maturity:** diagnose whether the client has no design practice, cosmetic styling only, problem-solving design, design that drives behaviour, or experience innovation; propose the next level, not the last.
- **Practical rules:** put the key action on the screen where it is needed, not in a menu; watch how experienced users work around the system to learn what to build; test how colours and shapes are read, because the same colour can signal warning in one shape and boundary in another.

## 7. Dashboards and data presentation

Charts exist to improve understanding: choose the chart for the data and the decision, avoid 3D and decorative effects, use colour for meaning, give context, and use size, colour, orientation and proximity deliberately. Visual execution routes to `design-system-skills`; dashboard specification to `meta-dashboard-design`.

## 8. Handoff

Record the scope level, the lens tensions, the outcome requirements and the validation plan in the brief. Implementation belongs to `website-skills`; visual design to `design-system-skills`; the ad-to-page journey contract to `advertising/ad-to-site-journey-handoff`.
