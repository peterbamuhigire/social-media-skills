# Offer, Proposition and Price Integrity — Working Reference

Read this when designing an offer, setting or defending a price, planning a discount, or writing the price section of a sales asset, rate card or proposal. Pair it with `biz-dev-pricing-menu` for agency packages. Route margin, tax and costing questions to `chwezi-accounting-doctrine`.

## Inputs

| Input | Source | If absent |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to deliver and required return | Client finance owner | Price range only; mark as provisional |
| Competitor and substitute prices | `meta-competitor-analysis`, field checks | Record as not assessed |
| Buyer segments and what they value | Interviews, sales notes | Run a short discovery before pricing |
| Proof, guarantees and bonuses available | Client | Do not promise them |

## 1. Diagnose the price problem first

Check, and write down, which apply: prices copied from formulas, habit or "the industry rate"; fear of cheaper rivals driving decisions; customers attracted mainly by low price; the owner's fixed belief about what buyers will pay; offers easy to compare like-for-like; weak differentiation; no premium option to signal quality; weak command of the business arithmetic; low confidence about charging more. Most price trouble is attitude before tactics — agree the diagnosis with the client before recommending a number.

## 2. Set the price

1. Start from the return the business needs (work back from target profit and volume); treat guessing, copying the norm, letting customers dictate, and plain cost-plus as starting points to avoid.
2. Adjust for the value delivered and for the alternatives the buyer actually considers.
3. Choose a direction over time: launch high and step down for early adopters, or launch low for share — the second is hard to climb out of.
4. In a downturn, test holding or raising price while improving value and marketing harder to the right buyers, rather than cutting by reflex — a hypothesis for finance to model, not a rule.
5. Remember that price signals quality: an implausibly low price can suggest counterfeit or corner-cutting, a real concern in Ugandan and Kenyan markets.

## 3. Build the propositions (Kennedy's five propositions)

| Proposition | Buyer's question | Build it from |
|---|---|---|
| Distinctive reason to choose | Why you rather than any alternative? | Positioning, a category of one, benefit-led copy |
| Value beyond price | Why is this worth far more than it costs? | Bundling, return evidence, intangible benefits |
| Hard-to-refuse offer | Why say yes now? | Useful bonuses, early-response bonus, a real deadline |
| Safety | Why is trying it low-risk? | Guarantee, warranty, peer proof |
| Experience | What is it like before, during and after? | Service moments, ritual, theme |

Lead with the strongest for the market and support it with the others. Test question: why should the buyer choose you regardless of price?

## 4. Escape the like-for-like comparison

- Sell the story, identity and emotion around the product, not only the object; name the method, the product and any ritual.
- Aim for a category of one; bundle differently; change one small element (delivery, packaging, guarantee, speed); create a named, proprietary process.
- Frame for a niche: the same product shaped for a specific trade or identity can justify a higher price at no extra production cost, provided it genuinely serves that niche (for example Bugisu arabica sold to specialty cafés rather than as commodity export grade).
- Never race to be second-cheapest.
- Borrow standing from the company you keep: client logos (with permission), media appearances, awards, venues and partner institutions.
- Shape what buyers expect to pay before the quote: where you advertise, the quality of the website and office, staff presentation, proposal packaging.

**Three sources of price power** (after Kennedy and Marrs' preeminence idea): expertise and service that resist comparison; visible marketing in the buyer's world; genuine community involvement. Keep more enquiries flowing than you can serve, so desperation never forces a discount.

## 5. Discount rules

- Every discount has a stated reason: volume, prepayment, season, pre-order, a behaviour you want (early registration), or a linked good cause.
- Get something back every time: early commitment, referrals, a longer term, larger volume.
- Discounts can lower perceived quality; use them sparingly and unpredictably (vary timing, reason and size so buyers do not wait for them).
- If discounts are needed to win new customers, confine them to one designated entry product.
- Never give a prestige offer away free; add value (warranty, bonus, financing, an event) instead of cutting price.

## 6. Competing with "free"

Make the hidden costs of the free option visible — queues, time, uncertain quality, loss of control, privacy. Seek buyers who pay for value and let price-only buyers go. Give away information that helps people decide; charge for delivery and results. This fits private-versus-public services in East Africa: schools, clinics, security, savings groups.

## 7. Protect the price internally

Under-pricing starves marketing, staff and product improvement and the damage compounds quietly. Remove unapproved authority to discount, tie incentives to gross margin, script price conversations and train staff to explain the price.

## 8. The buying committee

Household and B2B purchases usually involve several voices — the sceptic, the cost-controller, the enthusiast and the person who decides. Give each what they need to hear and close with the decider.

## 9. Bonuses and added value

A genuinely useful bonus can close a difficult sale, support retention and referrals, and is sometimes co-funded by a supplier. Low-cost additions — templates, checklists, an audit, a strategy call — can add real value. Present them honestly and never invent their worth.
