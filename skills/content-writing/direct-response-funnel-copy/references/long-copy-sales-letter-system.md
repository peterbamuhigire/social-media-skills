# Long-Copy Sales Letter System — Working Reference

Read this when drafting or auditing a long sales letter, long landing page, video sales script, long email or a WhatsApp broadcast sequence that must sell. Apply the [direct-marketing ethics filter](../../references/direct-marketing-ethics-filter.md) throughout. For offer design and price tactics use [offer, proposition and price integrity](offer-proposition-and-price-integrity.md); for funnel context use [funnel architecture and scripts](funnel-architecture-and-scripts.md).

## Inputs

| Input | Source | If absent |
|---|---|---|
| Reader profile in their own words | Interviews, reviews, WhatsApp enquiries, sales-call notes | Run the reader research in stage 1 before drafting |
| Offer, price, guarantee and any real deadline | Client, approved offer sheet | Placeholders only; do not invent terms |
| Consented proof (testimonials, results, data) | Client records with consent | Draft without proof and flag the gap |
| Channel and format | Brief | Default to the channel's native skeleton (stage 7) |

## Stage 1 — Research the reader before writing

Answer in writing, using the reader's own phrases wherever possible:
- What occupies them at the start and end of the day; what worries them; what they hope for.
- What is changing around them (prices, regulation, technology, family pressures).
- What they say at work, at home and among friends — and what they want but rarely admit.
- How they tend to decide (numbers-first, status-sensitive, cautious) and which words they use.
- Who else is selling to them and how; who has tried to help them and failed, and why.
- The hidden motive (what they really want from the result), any false belief blocking action ("I need a big shop to sell online"), their main fear, and what delivery method they will actually trust after past disappointments.

Picture the exact reading moment — a phone in a matatu queue, the office inbox at 8 a.m. — and write to it. If you are outside the audience, read their trade media, attend their events and listen to their conversations. For institutional buyers, answer the questions their own boards ask: what the organisation gains, who else has already committed, and which budget the money comes from.

**Hidden benefit.** People buy what a product does for them, not what it is. List every feature with its benefit, sort by importance to the buyer, and look for the non-obvious outcome that matters most — time back, standing among peers, avoiding embarrassment, a better start for the children. It is usually found in informal customer talk, not on the specification sheet.

## Stage 2 — Build the offer and the admission

Settle the offer before the copy (see the offer reference). Then decide the honest concession: name the obvious drawback and why it does not matter, or who the offer is not for ("We are not the cheapest caterer in Kampala; here is what the price covers." / "This course is for owners already selling at least weekly."). Unspoken flaws become silent doubts.

## Stage 3 — Draft against the skeleton

Write a fast, complete first draft without editing, then shape it to this skeleton:

```
HEADLINE                 → stops the right reader
OPENING PROMISE          → one specific promise (the box readers see first)
STORY / PROBLEM          → "I understand your situation"
HONEST CONCESSION        → pre-empts scepticism
PROMISE + CORE IDEA      → what they get and why it works
PROOF                    → consented, current testimonials, data, demonstrations
OFFER + VALUE            → product, bonuses, terms
PRICE HANDLING           → comparison, cost per use, payment options
GUARANTEE                → lawful, honoured risk reversal
REAL DEADLINE / LIMIT    → why act now, with the reason
CALL TO ACTION           → one clear route when selling
SIGNATURE                → a real, named person
P.S. LINES (1–3)         → each with one job
```

**Offer-led variant.** When the offer itself is the strongest asset, open with the offer block directly under the headline, place testimonials near the top to meet the doubt a big offer creates, then problem, solution and details, benefits, price (always stated), guarantee, a true limit, a benefits recap and a direct close. This ordering follows Serling's advice that the offer carries the piece.

**Persuasion devices to use deliberately:** attention–interest–desire–action, adding a conviction step for high-stakes decisions; problem–agitate–solve, agitating only pain the reader already feels; before and after; "so that" chains from feature to benefit to deeper benefit; bridge lines that pull into the next paragraph ("Here is why that matters…"); exact numbers over round ones (only when true); a reader-centred voice — count "you" against "we".

## Stage 4 — Handle price and objections

- List every reason the reader might not act; answer each with a story or specific proof, not a generic reassurance.
- Price: change the comparison (a different category, not a cheaper rival), give cost per day or per use, stack substantiated value, offer instalments (mobile-money plans where the provider permits), discount only in exchange for something (early decision, referral, larger order), frame the fee against a defensible result estimate, and state the price openly.

## Stage 5 — Create urgency honestly

Use only what is true, with the reason: a dated deadline, a proven limited quantity, a bonus for early response, a real consequence of waiting (the price does rise, the cohort does close), and a strong guarantee. Offer a choice of response channels when generating enquiries; give one clear route when selling.

**P.S. lines.** The P.S. is read early and often. Give each one job — restate the offer, add a bonus, restate the deadline, add a consented testimonial, or answer the biggest objection — in a warm, conversational tone.

## Stage 6 — Edit and test before release

1. Rewrite for strategy (right points, right order), then for style (voice, rhythm, readability).
2. Strip hedges and add conviction; bring in a true story and a human voice.
3. Recheck emphasis (subheads, bold, callouts) so a skimmer gets the whole argument; route visual execution to `design-system-skills`.
4. Compare with strong examples in the category (structure only, never wording).
5. Read aloud; send a mock-up to yourself on the real device; leave it 24–48 hours; get opinions from people who resemble the buyer.
6. Run the skeleton and the ethics filter as a final checklist; decide format, tracking and links.
7. Pre-test with a small send, then launch only with explicit client authority to send or publish.

## Stage 7 — Sequence and channel mapping

One message is rarely enough. Standard three-message sequence: full offer with deadline → "did this reach you?" with the most common objection answered and the offer restated → final notice with the strongest honest incentive. Stop on opt-out. Works in email, direct mail, SMS and WhatsApp within consent and platform policy.

| Channel | Mapping |
|---|---|
| Email | Subject line = envelope; preview text = opening promise; first two sentences = the hook |
| Landing page | The skeleton above, paced for scrolling; route build to `advertising/ad-to-site-journey-handoff` |
| Video sales script | Hook → story → problem → solution → offer → value → honest urgency → call to action → P.S. after the main call |
| Webinar | See [funnel architecture and scripts](funnel-architecture-and-scripts.md) |

## East African application (qualified)

- Serious purchases (land, school fees, insurance, equipment) are often researched carefully; long copy can work when it answers real concerns. Test length against a short control rather than assuming.
- A hand-delivered, addressed letter to a named B2B decision-maker in Kampala or Nairobi can be a strong format; route production to design and courier logistics.
- Multi-day sequences usually suit a relationship-based market better than a single broadcast; measure against your own control.
- Hidden benefits in the region often involve standing among peers, avoiding shame and giving children a better start; validate with interviews.
