# Funnel Architecture and Scripts — Working Reference

Read this when planning a selling funnel (course, coaching, high-ticket service, membership, event or webinar) or drafting any long-form script, launch sequence, post-purchase offer, live presentation or call close in `direct-response-funnel-copy`. Every step passes the [direct-marketing ethics filter](../../references/direct-marketing-ethics-filter.md). Every conversion rate here is a planning assumption to be replaced by the client's own test data; none is a benchmark.

## Inputs

| Input | Source | If absent |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer definition, where they gather, what attracts them, the end offer | Client brief, `03-audience-personas` | Run the funnel-design questions in section 1 with the client before drafting |
| Offer range and prices | Client, `biz-dev-pricing-menu` | Mark the ladder provisional |
| Revenue target and period | Client finance owner | Plan activity only; no revenue claims |

## 1. Funnel design decisions

**Design questions (answer in writing before any copy).** Who precisely is the buyer (role, size, situation, place)? Where do they already gather (lists, groups, associations, events, WhatsApp communities)? What would they gladly exchange contact details for (a guide, checklist, quiz, sample, short diagnostic)? Which offer is the funnel ultimately built to sell? Brunson calls this set his "secret formula"; the engine uses it as a pre-brief gate.

**Revenue maths, worked backwards.**
1. State the monthly revenue the funnel must produce (UGX).
2. Choose the model: recurring (price × members), back-end (price × sales) or a mix.
3. Divide back to the entry-offer buyers needed at an assumed upgrade rate.
4. Divide back to the visitors needed at an assumed entry conversion rate.
5. Compare with realistic traffic for the budget and channel. If the numbers cannot close, change the offer, the price or the model — not the effort level.

*Illustrative (Kampala coaching programme, all rates assumed):* UGX 12m a month from a UGX 600,000 programme needs 20 buyers; at an assumed 1 in 10 entry buyers upgrading, 200 entry buyers; at an assumed 4% entry conversion, about 5,000 visitors a month. If the ad budget buys far fewer qualified visits, redesign before spending.

**Offer ladder.** Arrange offers so each step serves the customer better: free entry item → low-cost starter → core offer → premium → recurring support. This is the value ladder (Brunson). Guard-rails: an entry offer must be a genuine diagnostic path into the core offer, never a cheap copy that traps buyers at the bottom (see `playbook-agency-operations`); the same knowledge may carry different prices only when the packaging adds real value such as implementation help, access or speed.

**Traffic ownership.** Classify every source: paid (rented), algorithmic, search or press reach (borrowed), and consented email, WhatsApp or SMS lists and customers (owned). Every rented or borrowed contact needs a consented route into an owned list — never scraping or copied lists. Reach a partner's audience only by the partner sending to its own opted-in list. In East Africa the owned list is usually a WhatsApp opt-in list; follow `playbook-whatsapp-business` and the data-protection checks in the ethics filter.

**Lead temperature and progression.** Grade each source hot (your own list), warm (referred or partner) or cold (strangers). Hot traffic can take a direct offer; warm traffic needs a short bridge that frames the problem; cold traffic needs an education step (story, article, video) before any offer. Then plan how a lead progresses: subscribes → makes a first small purchase → shows readiness for a larger offer → deepens the relationship over time → moves to a closer channel (message → call → event → one-to-one) as trust grows.

**Awareness matching.**

| Reader state | Lead with |
|---|---|
| Does not know the problem | A story or scene that surfaces it |
| Knows the problem | A precise name for it and why it persists |
| Knows solutions exist | Your mechanism and how it compares |
| Knows your offer | Offer, proof and guarantee |
| Ready to buy | Terms, how to pay, the real deadline |

Name the reader's unspoken question and the one promise the offer delivers. Keep copy moving with short sentences and open loops; use check-in questions sparingly and sincerely ("Does that match what you see in your shop?").

**Choosing a funnel type and its parts.** Pick the simplest type that fits the offer: lead opt-in; free or low-cost book with delivery; live or recorded webinar; product launch; physical-product checkout; pay-after-value; application for high-ticket work; membership. Assemble only the parts it needs from: bridge page, opt-in page, exit offer, order page with optional add-on, one-time offer and lighter alternative, thank-you page, member area, application form, registration and replay pages, long sales letter or video, survey, message sequences and call scripts. Route build and page design to `advertising/ad-to-site-journey-handoff`.

**Small-batch funnel test.** Before scaling spend, send a fixed small batch of visitors (Brunson's "100-visitor test") and read the conversion at each step against targets set in advance from the break-even maths in [direct-response economics](../../../advertising/direct-response-economics/SKILL.md). Fix the weakest step first; a strong opt-in page cannot rescue a weak order page.

## 2. Voice and story

**Public persona.** Decide the stance of the person carrying the offer (Brunson's "attractive character"): the proven guide ("I have done this; follow me"), the explorer ("come with me while I try"), the investigator ("I studied the people who succeeded") or the reluctant helper ("I did not plan this, but here is what I learned"). Record a true backstory, short teaching stories, openly admitted weaknesses and a clear stance some people will disagree with. The person must approve every element.

**Story shapes to rotate.** Setback then recovery; the better way against the old habit (a category or practice, never a disparaged named competitor without legal review); before and after; a discovery; a non-obvious insight that is genuinely true; a customer's own account (consented).

## 3. Script patterns by job

### Long-form persuasion arc (sales letter, video, webinar pitch)

Build in this engine's order; each block has one job.

| Block | Job | Ethics check |
|---|---|---|
| Stop the right reader | Interrupt with a specific question or scene they recognise | No bait-and-switch |
| Name the desire and the stalled attempts | Show you understand what they want and what has not worked | Agitate only pain they already feel |
| One promise | The single outcome the offer delivers | Substantiable |
| Who is speaking and why | Backstory, the obstacle, the moment of realisation | True, approved |
| The mechanism | Why the usual approach fails and what works instead; the obstacle as a category or habit | "Not your fault" only when true |
| Evidence | Consented case stories, before/after, typical results | Typical, not cherry-picked |
| The offer | What they get, including practical benefits (ease, speed, "so that" outcomes) | Accurate description |
| Value and price | Items with substantiated values, then the price | Only real prices actually charged |
| Risk reversal | Guarantee they can use | Lawful and honoured |
| Honest urgency | A real deadline or limit, with its reason | See section 4 |
| Picture the result, then act | A realistic after-scene and one clear action | No income promises |
| After the call to action | What happens next; who it is not for; a closing reminder; one to three P.S. lines, each with one job | No pressure tactics |

### Launch and nurture sequences

- **Five-message story sequence after opt-in** (Brunson calls it a soap-opera sequence): welcome and what to expect → the story up to the obstacle → the realisation and the one idea → unexpected benefits → the offer with a real deadline. In East Africa, voice notes suit the story messages and a text-plus-image call to action suits the last, within WhatsApp Business policy and opt-in.
- **Three-message follow-up** (high-consideration and B2B, after Kennedy): full offer with deadline → "did this reach you?" plus the most common objection answered → final honest notice. Stop on request.
- **Ongoing notes:** rotate a small real incident, a lesson learned and a practical how-to, each with a light, relevant product link.

### Post-purchase one-time offer

Confirm the good decision and why it was sensible → ask whether a faster or fuller result would help → present one named additional mechanism → show the result realistically → call to action with guarantee → honest value summary and the real reason it is offered at this point → second call to action → consented testimonials. Offer it once; declining must take one click or reply.

### Live presentation or webinar (Perfect Webinar, Brunson)

Promise ("how to [result] without [pain]") → three teaching points, each introducing one idea and loosening one limiting belief → summary of what is included with substantiated values → price → closing moves chosen by the objection they answer → one clear action with sign-up help.

**Closing moves by buyer objection**

| Objection or hesitation | Move | Ethical rule |
|---|---|---|
| "There is more to this than one session covers" | Offer implementation help as an option; ask whether they want it | Never imply the free session was deliberately incomplete; accept "no" |
| "Is it worth the money?" | Recap each included item with its real value; ask whether one realistic outcome alone would justify it | Values substantiated; no income promises |
| "I cannot spend on this now" | Reframe as an investment only for buyers who can afford it; offer a smaller entry step | Never encourage borrowing; respect genuine constraints |
| "I can figure it out alone" | Contrast doing it alone with guided support; explain what support is actually delivered | Both paths presented as legitimate |
| "I have tried before" | Explain why information alone rarely changes behaviour and what is different here; offer the guarantee | Real guarantee only |
| "I keep putting it off" | Ask "What would need to be true for you to start?" | Shaming or "excuses" framing is banned |
| "Will it work for someone like me?" | A presenter who is not exceptional, typical results, a relevant case | Only if true; show typical outcomes |
| "What changes if I join?" | A realistic now-and-later picture | Evidence-based |
| Two or three specific doubts | Name the common ones and answer each with facts | Keep |
| "How do I actually join?" | Walk through the sign-up and payment steps | Always keep — it removes friction |
| Final decision | Restate the one core benefit and the single action | One call to action; any deadline or limit must be real |

Drop any move that mocks non-buyers, pressures debt or dismisses genuine constraints.

### High-ticket two-call close

**First call:** introduction; open questions; "perhaps I can help"; why the programme is selective (real criteria only); situation and goals; affordability check; four commitments — time, willingness to learn, the investment, and the decision-maker present. **Second call:** confirm each commitment, recap what they receive, agree the next step and the payment method (bank transfer or mobile money where the provider allows).

## 4. Honest urgency and anchors

- Values: state only real, substantiated values (a price actually charged, a documented cost). Never invent a "total value".
- Deadlines and limits: only true ones, with the reason (a cohort size set by the venue, a price change that will happen).
- Opposition: a category, habit or outdated method — not a named competitor or a group of people.
- Income, health or financial outcomes need substantiation and legal review.

## 5. Study, then test

Study offers already working in the market (for example current ads in the Meta Ad Library) before inventing. Run a control, then try to beat it one variable at a time; record every result.
