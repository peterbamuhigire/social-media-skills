# Capture for Repurposing — Content Production Around a Busy Expert

Read this before recording any interview, podcast, webinar or talking-head session that will be cut into clips, posts, articles and newsletters, and when designing a content service for a founder, partner or executive who has little time. Parent skill: [`meta-content-repurposing`](../SKILL.md).

## 1. Inputs and decisions

| Decision | Rule |
|---|---|
| What the expert must personally do | Only what nobody else can: approve the topic and speak on record. Everything else is delegated, automated or cut |
| Live or pre-recorded | Live only when the connection and moderation cover are reliable; otherwise record and upload later from a fixed connection, off-peak |
| Which outputs | Decide before recording — clip lengths, article angles, newsletter item — so the conversation produces them |
| When to stop polishing | At the quality floor (section 6); beyond it, publishing consistently matters more |

## 2. Design the workflow once

1. Write down every task from idea to promotion.
2. Tag each task: expert only / delegate / automate / drop.
3. Turn the result into a checklist or standard operating procedure so the expert makes fewer decisions and nothing is missed.
4. Assign roles — scheduler, editor, publisher — part-time is fine.
5. Automate hand-offs such as transcription and scheduling only after a data-protection check on client recordings (Uganda DPPA and Kenya DPA duties; register PL-01, PL-02).
6. Start with the simplest version (audio only) and add layers as the routine holds.

**Target expert time (engine working budget):** about half an hour a month approving topics, one or two recording sessions of under an hour, and a single weekly batch of approvals of about a quarter of an hour.

## 3. One session, many assets

| Stage | Actions |
|---|---|
| Before | Pick the topic from the pillar map; write five to seven questions designed for self-contained answers; brief the expert on framing (section 4); test the equipment |
| During | Record 30–45 minutes; keep a note of strong moments with timestamps |
| After | Checked transcript; full episode (video and audio); several short captioned clips; a highlights cut; a web page with summary, speaker bio, links and transcript; one or two articles edited from the transcript; a newsletter item; a WhatsApp Channel or broadcast snippet for opted-in audiences; quote cards |
| Release | Full episode first, then one clip a day for the working week, the highlights cut at the end of the week, newsletter on its usual day |
| Review | Monthly: which pieces drew enquiries or questions; mine those questions for next month's topics |

## 4. Recording so the material cuts cleanly

- **Self-contained answers:** one idea, one example, one takeaway per answer — roughly under a minute and a half spoken (engine working guide, not a platform rule).
- **Frame the answer so it stands alone:** the speaker repeats the question's subject in the first sentence ("The way small clinics cut no-shows is a WhatsApp reminder the evening before…"), so the clip needs no interviewer.
- **Brief for outputs:** tell the expert which clips and articles are planned so they give quotable, specific answers.

Useful question starters: "What is the biggest mistake [audience] makes with [topic], and what should they do instead?" · "If a [business type] in [town] had only [budget], where should they start?" · "What number should every [role] watch, and why?"

## 5. Recording set-up checklist

- [ ] Camera at eye level; the speaker looks into the lens.
- [ ] A dynamic USB microphone in ordinary rooms (it rejects more room noise than a condenser).
- [ ] Soft light in front of the speaker; no bright window behind.
- [ ] Curtains, rugs or cushions to cut echo.
- [ ] A tidy or branded background (visual rules via `design-system-skills`).
- [ ] A short test recording before any important call.

Fix audio first: published research found listeners judged the same content and speaker less favourably when sound quality was poor (Newman and Schwarz, 2018, *Science Communication*). Use this as a reason to prioritise audio, not as a performance figure.

## 6. Mine what already exists, and hold a quality floor

1. Collect the longest client emails and replies from the last six months and the questions that keep recurring on sales calls, in the WhatsApp Business inbox and at support.
2. Pick the five most frequent; title each in the buyer's own words.
3. A question asked often becomes a series, not a single post.
4. Turn internal procedures into behind-the-scenes trust content ("How we onboard a new school client, step by step").
5. Anonymise and obtain consent before reusing anyone's words.

Quality floor (non-negotiable): accurate facts, clear audio, brand fit, captions and accessibility. Above the floor, publish rather than polish; most quality comes from the research, the outline and the key messages.

## 7. Goals and reporting

Content can serve leads, audience growth, authority, relationships with prospective clients, recruitment and relationships with respected people in the field — name which ones apply. For B2B thought leadership, do not judge by last-click attribution. Report a body of work: assets published per pillar, share of planned slots published, expert minutes per published asset, conversations where a prospect cited a piece, speaking and media invitations, and applicants who mention the content. If the expert will not commit to content, the legitimate alternative is deliberate networking, referral requests and lawful outbound (`biz-dev-lawful-prospecting-outreach`).

**Worked example (illustrative):** a Kampala law-firm partner records one 40-minute conversation a month on changes that affect SME clients (facts checked by counsel). The team produces five LinkedIn clips, one article, a newsletter item and a WhatsApp Channel update; the partner's total time, including approvals, stays near an hour and a quarter.
