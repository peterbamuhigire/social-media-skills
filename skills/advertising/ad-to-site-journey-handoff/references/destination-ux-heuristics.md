# Destination UX heuristics

When to read: when briefing or auditing any page, form or app screen that receives campaign traffic. These are the engine's review heuristics for marketers, synthesised from several UX sources (listed at the end); implementation and visual design belong to website-skills and design-system-skills. Run the sections in order: first view, memory load, controls, placement, usability, scope, funnel, outcomes, review, trust, journey.

## 1. The first view decides whether the rest is read

- Visitors judge appeal and credibility in the first moments, on feeling rather than analysis; a cluttered, ugly or flashing page loses them before content is assessed.
- Once people have decided to buy from a merchant, they tolerate friction and defend their choice; appeal decides whether content is evaluated at all.
- Context changes preference: an audience that enjoys colour and motion on a festival page may distrust the same treatment on a loan or medical-booking page. Match the page's tone to the task the visitor came for.
- Brief implication: the first view must look credible for this audience and task, carry the ad's promise, and show one clear action.

## 2. Keep the memory load low

- Working memory holds only a few items briefly; interruptions wipe it. Keep choices few; group information into chunks (a phone number in blocks, steps in threes).
- Break long tasks into short steps with visible progress and frequent closure; never make a visitor hold information from one screen to the next.
- Recognition over recall: let visitors choose from visible options rather than remember codes, prices or instructions.
- Returning experts value shortcuts; first-time visitors need visible cues.

## 3. Check every call to action in four stages

| Stage | Question | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Presence | Does the cue exist (label, format hint, default, reminder of next step)? | Unlabelled icons; forms with no format hints ("clueless consent") |
| Visibility | Can it be seen: rendered, not hidden, not lost in clutter? | Sign-in or WhatsApp button tucked in a corner; messages in a status bar nobody reads |
| Recognisability | Is it found without searching: location, contrast, size, separation? | CTA same style as body text |
| Intelligibility | Is it legible and is its meaning clear? | Vague labels ("Submit", "Click here") |

Failure at any stage breaks the action. Use the visitor's familiar words (sign up, book, get the price) rather than invented labels.

## 4. Put the key action where it is needed

People rarely explore menus; if they cannot do what they came for on the screen they are on, they leave. Put the key action beside the thing it acts on. Observe how experienced users actually work: their workarounds show what to put on screen.

## 5. Usability qualities and responsiveness

| Component | Page check |
|---|---|
| Learnability | Can a first-time visitor complete the task without instructions? |
| Efficiency | How many steps and fields does the task need? |
| Memorability | Can a returning visitor find it again? |
| Errors | Are errors prevented, and handled politely with a clear fix? |
| Satisfaction | Does the flow feel smooth rather than forced? |

Responsiveness: confirm every submission; show loading states; never leave a blank page or a dead end; every page carries a contact route. Consistency: similar tasks work the same way; the logo returns home.

## 6. Declare the scope level

Declare which level the journey work covers so expectations match: single interaction (this page or form), journey (ad → page → WhatsApp → payment → confirmation over time), or relationship (the whole experience with the organisation). Most "scope creep" disputes come from a client expecting relationship-level change from a single-page brief.

## 7. Design each engagement level with a measurable question

Design for conversion at each engagement level, with a measurable learning question per level:

| Level | Visitor process | Business task | Required functionality | Measurable question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Suspect | Lands from an ad or search | Convey the proposition fast | Matching landing page | What share of each source reaches the key section? |
| Lead | Leaves contact details or starts a chat | Capture and respond | Form or click-to-chat; response process | Cost per lead by source; response time |
| Prospect | Engages with offer, demo, quote | Qualify and nurture | Follow-up sequence, booking | Lead-to-prospect rate |
| Customer | Buys or books | Close and deliver | Payment, confirmation | Prospect-to-customer rate; cost per customer |
| Reference | Reviews, refers, shares | Encourage advocacy | Review and referral asks with consent | Referral and review rate |

"How intrigued were visitors?" is not measurable; "what share of paid-social visitors reached the pricing section?" is.

## 8. Five-outcome launch gate

A destination must be: Useful (addresses the visitor's real goal), Easy (no training, one clear action), Efficient (fast to scan and complete), Pleasing (quality consistent with the brand's positioning) and Accessible (WCAG 2.2 AA target, CW-05). A "no" on any outcome blocks go-live.

## 9. Heuristic review (widely used Nielsen-style list)

Visibility of system status · match with the real world (visitor's words) · user control and freedom (back, undo, edit) · consistency and standards · error prevention and handling · recognition rather than recall · flexibility and efficiency · aesthetic and minimal design · help and documentation. Score each pass / fix / not assessed with evidence.

## 10. Trust cues for East African destinations

- A visible way to reach a human reassures because visitors *can* call, even if few do; callers are high-intent. In Uganda and Kenya show WhatsApp click-to-chat, a dialable +256/+254 number, a physical landmark address and a response-time promise.
- Remove low counters (few reviews, low follower numbers) until they persuade; never manufacture social proof.
- Usability is hygiene and persuasion the motivator: fix speed, clarity and mobile layout before adding persuasion devices.

## 11. Map journeys that cross channels

For journeys crossing channels, map phases left to right (discover → evaluate → buy → use → advocate) with rows for thinking, feeling, activities, touchpoints, questions asked, and friction or missing capability. Layer the business's service steps under the customer's view to find where a handoff breaks.
