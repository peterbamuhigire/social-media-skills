# Campaign structure and copy–image rules

When to read: once a platform idea is chosen and the team must decide how units relate to each other and how headline and visual work together. Visual execution itself routes to `design-system-skills`.

## 1. Triplets vs cousins

| Structure | What stays fixed | Use for | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triplets | Master template: type alignment, element positions, palette, typefaces, image style; only image and headline change | High-frequency, short-exposure media: OOH, transit and taxi branding, paid social prospecting, retail point of sale | Later units bore people who have seen one |
| Cousins | Family resemblance; composition, palette and visualisation vary within limits ("unity with variety") | Long-running organic social, editorial content, brand campaigns where fatigue matters more | Drift that loses recognition |

In both cases lock the distinctive brand assets (logo, colour, type, tone, sound) as the red thread, and keep one imagery treatment across all units (all photographic or all illustrated; one lighting style).

## 2. Integrated campaign checklist

- Rests on one flexible platform idea.
- Every unit grabs and holds attention and compels one action.
- Each unit is conceived for its channel: one message, native execution (long video for broad storytelling; OOH, feed and print for a quicker single message).
- Art direction distinguishes the brand from competitors.
- It starts a conversation people would continue.
- It offers entertainment, information, utility or social good.
- Each unit is tagged brand-love or transaction, and judged accordingly.

## 3. Copy–image constructions

Principle: headline and image together communicate more than either alone. Cover-the-headline test and cover-the-image test: neither alone should carry the whole message. Never be redundant (if you show a horse, don't write "horse").

| Construction | Description | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Copy-driven | Headline or typography is the star | Urban professionals on LinkedIn and X; ideas that live in words |
| Image-driven | The image carries the message; the line contextualises | Multilingual and mixed-literacy audiences across East Africa |
| Emblematic | Image and words fused and inseparable | Memorable single-frame ideas, OOH |

Straight/crooked rule: if the image is odd, funny or shocking, keep the copy straight, and vice versa. Both straight = dull; both clever = they fight.

Additional rules from direct-response practice (Serling 2002 contributors):
- Every ad should work as a poster: headline plus visual carry the selling idea without body copy.
- Product as hero on a plain background often beats "borrowed interest" scenes that need a strained bridge headline; test it.
- Use visuals the audience relates to (their trade, their setting, people like them).
- The eye goes image → caption, so a caption should restate the benefit.
- A friendly face beside a real, consented quote is a reusable layout.
- Upper and lower case headlines; reverse type only for headlines, never body copy.
- Editorial-style layouts must be clearly labelled as advertising.

For radio-heavy markets, the "image" is sound design: a distinctive sonic device plays the red-thread role.

## 4. Creative hand-off to design-system-skills

Hand over: brief, chosen platform idea, idea card, campaign structure decision, copy–image construction per unit, mandatory assets and disclosures, channel list with current format specs (see the paid-social and paid-search skills for register-checked specs), accessibility needs (captions, alt text, contrast), and approval owner. Do not specify fonts or colours here; the design engine governs type and visual identity.
