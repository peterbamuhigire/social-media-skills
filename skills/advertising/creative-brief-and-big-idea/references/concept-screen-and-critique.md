# Concept screen and critique protocol

When to read: after ideation and before any concept reaches the client; before a creative review meeting; when feedback is drifting into taste.

## 1. Idea card (one per concept)

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Insight | Human truth + brand truth, with evidence note |
| Lodestar line | One sentence: what people should think or feel + the mechanism |
| Benefit type | Social good / utility / information / temptation / entertainment / shareworthy |
| Human driver and brand role | From the story spine |
| Press-release headline + two-line story | What culture would report if the idea landed |
| WhatsApp-forward version (East Africa) | One image + two lines that survive forwarding |
| Three channel sketches | e.g. short video, OOH/transit, experiential or radio |
| Brand-love or transaction unit? | Tag each unit |
| Likely negative reactions | Named, with planned response |

## 2. Internal screen (before the client sees anything)

1. State the brief's objective and audience aloud.
2. Each reviewer scores each concept independently on the seven-point effectiveness scale before discussion:
   1 destructive · 2 pedestrian · 3 off-brand · 4 not an idea · 5 on-brand but not attention-getting · 6 attention-getting short term · 7 strategically creative.
3. Idea-quality grid (0–2 each; internal checklist only): context (fits daily routines), humanistic (adds good), observational (grounded in behaviour), interesting (holds attention), craft (execution potential), empathy (grounded in needs).
4. Acceptance questions: does it attract; is there a lodestar; is it timely; does it engage; does it resonate; is it shareworthy; does it tantalise with a named emotion and a call to action?
5. Responsible-creative interrogation (section 5).
6. Decide: kill 1–4; send 5 back for a sharper hook; advance 6–7. Flag score spreads greater than 2 for discussion.

For hard direct-response work, weight offer clarity and proof above "interesting" and "context".

## 3. Test pack

| Test | When | Method (SME-feasible) | Reads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value-proposition / message test | Before execution | Show 2–3 benefit statements to 30–50 target people (WhatsApp poll, intercept) or a small split of plain-text ads | Which benefit wins; label as directional, not normed |
| Execution test | After message is chosen | Small-budget paid split, or pre-test with recall, key-message playback, likeability, uniqueness to advertiser | Winner and why |
| Brand-linkage diagnostic | Any execution | Ask unprompted: what was it for; who was it from? | Liked-but-not-linked pattern (high likeability, low message playback or brand attribution) → fix branding and single message, not media weight |

Route the statistical design and decision rules to [meta-testing-framework](../../../meta-analytics-ops/meta-testing-framework/SKILL.md) and the paid test build to [ad-testing-and-scaling](../../ad-testing-and-scaling/SKILL.md). Testing validates and mitigates; it rarely chooses the most distinctive idea, because respondents favour the familiar.

## 4. Review meeting protocol

Before: circulate the brief; confirm the decision-maker attends; agree the five design criteria (on brand; stands out yet relevant; timeless; practical across today's and tomorrow's touchpoints; excites organisation and consumer).

Presenting (re-frame first): spend about half the time on the assignment — task given, approach, who we spoke to, what we found, what we tried — then show the work with visible conviction. Never open with "Well, what do you think?". Close with "Next steps: review, select a direction, don't agonise."

Receiving (percolate, then discuss):
1. Do not decide in the room.
2. The client team takes the work away for a day or two.
3. Return for an hour-long discussion of what each idea *is* and its likely negative reactions.
4. Confirm everyone, including the creators, agrees what the idea is.
5. Spend time on why an idea *can* work before listing why it can't.
6. Decide; record sign-off on likely negative reactions for provocative work.

Feedback rules:
- Say the problem, not the solution ("the brand arrives too late", not "make the logo bigger").
- Give the reason and what works as well as what doesn't.
- Use terminology: insight, platform idea, visual hierarchy, focal point, palette, voice, call to action.
- Separate off-brief (reject), on-brief but weak (iterate) and taste (drop).
- Block inappropriate feedback: dictatorial changes without reason; feedback from people who have not read the brief; feedback that changes the brief mid-stream; feedback that demotes creatives to production; relayed opinions of absent third parties.
- Taste self-check: am I reacting to the palette from personal preference or because it fits the idea, brand, audience and channel? Do I prefer this because it is familiar? The brand positioning sets the taste level, captured in an agreed mood board.

Feedback phrasing, before → after:

| Generic | Professional |
|---|---|
| "That image is ugly." | "The palette drifts from the brand's greens; can we test it on-palette?" |
| "Make the logo bigger." | "Recall checks show weak brand linkage; can the brand appear in the first two seconds?" |
| "I don't like the music." | "The research says this audience responds to [genre]; does this track fit them?" |
| "Make it like [competitor]'s ad." | "What are we responding to in that reference: the warmth, the colour, the confidence? Let's bring that quality in a way nobody mistakes for theirs." |

## 5. Responsible-creative interrogation

Images and copy: how might different communities react; is any stereotype or trope present (ability, ethnicity, tribe, gender, religion, age, class); is an unchangeable trait a punchline; swap test (swap the depicted group — does a stereotype appear?); outside perspective sought?

Power: who holds power in the image; does it punch up or down; does it read well from several perspectives?

Appropriation: whose story is it; is sacred tradition respected; would members of that culture represent themselves this way; is credit given?

East Africa trope list to check: poverty or helplessness imagery common in development communication; ethnic or tribal humour; stereotyped portrayals of rural people, house helps and boda-boda riders; colourism and skin-lightening aesthetics; religious sensitivities; household gender roles; party colours and political symbolism in election periods; disability shown only as "inspiration". Content touching sexuality in Uganda requires a legal check before release (law and enforcement not assessed in this engine).

Social responsibility scale: damaging → ignorant → needs scrutiny → provocative (takes a side, not inflammatory) → intelligent and respectful → does good. Only the top three may ship.

Purpose-led claims: mission-level, funded with money or action, sustained over years, backlash anticipated and signed off; performative gestures without action are rejected.
