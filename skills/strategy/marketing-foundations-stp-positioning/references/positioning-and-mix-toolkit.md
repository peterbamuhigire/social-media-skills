# Positioning and marketing-mix toolkit

Read this when writing or testing a positioning statement, drawing a perceptual map, choosing a value discipline, or setting the marketing mix for a client programme. Parent skill: [marketing-foundations-stp-positioning](../SKILL.md).

## 1. STP procedure

1. **Segment.** Group customers by needs, buying behaviour and current alternative. Describe each group by demographics, location (district, town, estate) and payment habit (mobile money, cash, card, bank). People-based and product-based segmentation can both be used; say which.
2. **Qualify each segment.** Answer yes/no with evidence:
   - Can we identify and reach it (lists, media, channels, associations)?
   - Is it large enough to pay back the effort this year?
   - Will it pay the intended price?
   - Does tailoring the offer or message cost less than the extra sales it can bring? If the tailoring cost is not clearly below the incremental sales, merge or drop the segment (Stockwell & Shaw, 1994).
3. **Target.** Choose one primary segment and, only if capacity allows, one secondary segment with a trigger date. Write the excluded groups and the reason.
4. **Position.** Write the statement (section 2), map the choice set (section 4) and test it (section 3).

## 2. Statement template and proof discipline

> For [target and mindset], [Brand] is the [frame of reference or category] that [single promise], because [reason to believe].

- The frame of reference is what the buyer compares you with, not what you call yourself.
- The reason to believe must be a checkable fact: a certification, a process, a named partnership, a published result with source and date. An adjective is not proof.
- One label per brand. The mind files a brand under one idea; a statement that tries to say everything is remembered for nothing (Kelley & Sheehan, c. 2021–22).

## 3. Tests before approval

**Seven questions** (all must be "yes"):

1. Does it differentiate the brand from the named alternatives?
2. Does it match a truth customers recognise?
3. Is the value to the customer clear?
4. Is it memorable in one reading?
5. Can every channel and touchpoint use it consistently?
6. Is it believable and provable?
7. Can the brand own it over time?

**Four failure modes** (none may be present):

| Failure | Symptom | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Under-positioning | Buyers cannot say what the brand stands for | Choose one promise and repeat it everywhere |
| Over-positioning | Position too narrow for current awareness | Widen the frame of reference or the target |
| Confused positioning | Different channels say different things | One statement, one message sheet, audited per touchpoint |
| Double positioning | Claim too close to a stronger competitor | Move to an attribute the brand can prove and the rival does not own |

**Trout-style sequence:** map what competitors already own → find the differentiating idea in the brand's real strengths → confirm it can be supported by facts for years → promote it.

## 4. Choice set and perceptual map

1. List what the buyer actually considers: direct competitors, substitutes in other categories and "do nothing". Dessert can be ice cream or a home-baked cake; a school's rival can be a boarding school two districts away.
2. Choose two attributes that genuinely drive choice (from interviews or reviews, not from the brand's wish list).
3. Plot each option. Read the white space and ask whether the brand can credibly own it.
4. Record the evidence behind each plotted position. A map built on opinion is a hypothesis, labelled as such.

## 5. Value discipline

Choose one discipline to excel at and hold threshold standards on the other two (Treacy & Wiersema, *The Discipline of Market Leaders*, as applied in Kelley & Sheehan):

| Discipline | The brand wins on | Marketing implication |
|---|---|---|
| Operational excellence | Price, speed, reliability, ease | Proof of efficiency; simple offers; clear prices |
| Product leadership | Newest, best-performing offer | Demonstration, expert proof, launch cadence |
| Customer intimacy | Tailored solutions and relationships | Case stories, account management, testimonials about service |

Porter's warning applies: a brand stuck between low cost and differentiation usually loses to both.

## 6. Marketing mix

| Element | Decision to record | Contradiction check |
|---|---|---|
| Product / offer | Core offer, bundle, guarantee, service level | Does the offer deliver the promise? |
| Price | Position (premium, parity, below), payment terms, instalments | Does the price signal match the position? |
| Place | Where and how people buy (shop, WhatsApp, delivery, agents) | Can the target buy easily in their location? |
| Promotion | Role of each channel (build, harvest, retain) | Do all channels carry one message? |
| People (services) | Who serves, their training and conduct | Do staff behave as the position promises? |
| Process (services) | Booking, response time, delivery steps | Are friction points removed? |
| Physical evidence | Premises, uniforms, packaging, website, documents | Does every "slice" look like the position? |

**Five F's message line** (Abrams): write a sentence for each of Functions (how it meets the need), Finances (effect on the buyer's whole financial position), Freedom (time and worry saved), Feelings (how the buyer feels about themselves and the seller) and Future (support and security later). Choose the one or two that most motivate the target and stake out a competitive position. In East Africa, "Finances" often means cash-flow friendliness (instalments, mobile money, smaller pack sizes); "Future" includes after-sales presence upcountry and confidence the firm will still exist.

## 7. Point of view over USP

A unique selling proposition is easily copied; a point of view endures (Inamoto, interviewed in Landa, 2022). Answer two questions in the brief: What differentiates the brand? Why does it do what it does?

## 8. Competitive tactics (for challengers and leaders)

Offensive options (Wheelen & Hunger): frontal (needs superior resources), flanking (attack where the rival is weak), bypass (change the rules with a new format or route), encirclement (wider range or more markets), guerrilla (small, intermittent moves that do not force the leader to respond). Defensive options: raise switching costs honestly (training, service, integration), respond with concentrated force rather than token moves, keep costs down. Map the chosen tactic to a digital campaign role and check comparative claims with the legal gate.

**Illustration (scenario):** a new specialty-coffee roaster does not attack a supermarket leader head-on. It flanks through offices and co-working spaces, runs guerrilla tasting pop-ups, and bypasses retail with a WhatsApp subscription and mobile-money auto-pay.
