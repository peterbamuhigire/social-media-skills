# Key-account planning and negotiation

Read when a client (or an agency retainer) is strategic enough to deserve a dedicated plan, when reviewing relationship health, or when preparing a renewal or price negotiation. Parent skill: [strategy-b2b-customer-community](../SKILL.md). Frameworks come from Marcos, J., Guesalaga, R., Hough, A. and Vincent, R. (c. 2025) *The High-Performing Key Account Manager*, Kogan Page, and the research it cites. Only its research-backed frameworks are used here; the book's uncited percentages and named "institutes" must never be quoted.

## 1. Choosing key accounts

Select on strategic importance, not size alone: profit-growth potential, reference value, market access, willingness to innovate, and your current strength in the account. Review annually and be willing to remove key status. Keep "next flagship" candidates.

**McDonald's four account types** (axes: potential for profit growth × supplier strength):

| Type | Position | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | High potential, high strength | Senior business manager, joint planning, multi-function contact |
| Star | High potential, lower strength | Entrepreneurial pursuit and investment to build position |
| Status | Lower potential, high strength | Delivery discipline and margin protection |
| Streamline | Low potential, low strength | Cost-to-serve control; productised tier or managed exit |

In family-owned groups, strength often rests on personal access to the owner: score relationship strength per stakeholder, not only per company.

## 2. Decoding the customer's pragmatic strategy

1. Read formal sources: annual report, investor statements, regulator filings, published strategy.
2. Interview several levels, including operators.
3. Use trade-off methods beyond surveys: best–worst (MaxDiff) ranking of attributes; observation or structured interviews to surface hidden needs.
4. If stakeholders disagree, help them converge: give each ten tokens to spread across priorities (quality, delivery time, cost) and discuss the pattern.
5. Answer five questions: business objectives; market position and competition; structure and decision process; pain points; future vision.

Plan for both deliberate and emergent strategy. If directions cannot be agreed, part ways rather than drift into work where you add no value.

## 3. Buying-centre map (GRASP)

For each person in the decision-making unit (initiator, user, influencer, gatekeeper, decider, budget controller, buyer) record:

| G — Goal | R — Role | A — Appeal | S — State | P — Power |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What we want with this person and our action | DMU role(s) | What appeals to them in the offer and process | Current relationship | Level and source of power |

Check multi-threading: count active relationships by level (executive, director, manager, operator) and function; flag single-threaded accounts and accounts with no senior tie.

## 4. Customer analysis tools

- **9-box SWOT:** start from the customer's objectives, run a SWOT, then derive Attack (strengths + opportunities), Convert (weaknesses + opportunities), Defend (strengths + threats) and Block (weaknesses + threats) moves.
- **Competitive profile matrix:** weight critical success factors to 1.0, rate each supplier 1–4, sum weighted scores.
- **Financial diagnosis in the customer's language:** revenue index by segment (base year = 100); cash conversion cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days; working capital released ≈ days cut × annual revenue ÷ 365 (for the relevant share); operating margin; return on capital employed. Validate formulas with the finance engine.

Scenario: a manufacturer with a cash conversion cycle of 80 days on UGX 36bn annual revenue (≈ UGX 99m a day) frees about UGX 2bn if the cycle falls by 20 days. Present that to the finance director instead of "better visibility".

## 5. Relationship position and quality

**Typology** (dependency × exchange style): vulnerable supplier, vulnerable customer, inviting customer, inviting supplier, competitive interaction, collaborative partnership. Place each account, set a target position and timeline, and agree actions with the customer where possible. An agency earning a large share of revenue from one telecom client is a vulnerable supplier: diversify, build proprietary tools the client values, and move towards gain-sharing.

**Relationship quality = managed conflict + trust + commitment.** Drivers: shared goals and co-defined KPIs; information sharing (roadmaps, early disclosure of disruption); cooperation and reciprocity (shared upside and risk); personal ties. Survey both sides on a short agree/disagree scale across these drivers and compare.

## 6. Trust

- Components: ability, benevolence, integrity. When suppliers are equally credible, benevolence decides loyalty.
- Development routes: calculative (clear contracts), prediction (repeated reliability), capability (proof), intentionality (acts beyond the contract), transference (endorsements).
- **Trust equation** (Maister et al., *The Trusted Advisor*): credibility + reliability + intimacy, divided by self-orientation. Lower self-orientation by recommending the cheaper option when it fits and saying where you fall short.

**Trust-recovery protocol:** acknowledge within 24 hours and own it; state the impact; issue a resolution plan with steps, owners and dates; send progress updates; share a root-cause review; rebuild through consistent follow-through.

Script: "We missed [commitment]. The impact on you was [impact]. Here's what we're doing, who owns it, and when you'll hear from us next."

## 7. Value-based selling and co-creation

- Preconditions: multilevel relationships, room to tailor, a seller rewarded for how they sell.
- Three dimensions: understand the customer's business model; craft a tailored proposition; quantify value in the customer's metrics.
- Product pitch vs value pitch: replace feature lists with an estimate built from the customer's own data, a baseline and a measurement plan.
- **Four I co-creation model:** interaction, integration (pooled resources), investigation (joint diagnosis), innovation. Sequence: co-diagnose → co-ideate → co-design → co-test → co-evaluate. True co-creation needs comparable customer effort; check readiness first.
- **Value assessment** (Keränen & Jalkala): identify value potential → agree a baseline → evaluate performance → document realised value, which becomes the next proposal's case study.
- **Quarterly value-tracking agenda:** baseline recap → metrics moved → value realised → issues and recovery → new opportunities → joint priorities → decisions needed.

## 8. Negotiation and renewal

Before: prepare both sides' interests and constraints in ranges; plan risk- and profit-sharing honestly; start with compatible interests; prepare to disagree with reasons and questions rather than labels ("Here's what our delivery data shows… how would a [x]-week timeline protect your launch date?").

During:
- Value first, then price; move the conversation to total cost of ownership.
- Bring upstream or downstream parties in to lower total system cost rather than cutting unit price.
- **MESO:** present three packages of equal value to you that differ in price, scope, support and risk-sharing; note which the client leans to and why.
- Contingent agreements when value is uncertain (performance-linked terms).
- **Concessions:** plan them; trade what is cheap for you and valuable to them; label each as a concession; make it conditional ("If you commit to 12 months, then we hold current rates"); make each successive concession smaller; do not over-justify.
- Team roles: lead negotiator, spokesperson, analyst, observer, note-taker.

After: a **post-settlement session** — "Our agreement stands whatever happens in the next half hour. Can we look for anything that would make it better for both of us: timing, payment, extra services, introductions?"

Ethics: never exaggerate complexity to extract a price, and never slip terms past the buyer. Coercive levers (contract enforcement) are a documented last resort after internal alignment.

## 9. Executive sponsor

Research cited in the book finds senior involvement helps key-account effectiveness and that social interaction with the customer's leaders matters more than tactical intervention (Guesalaga, 2014). Sponsor roles include strategic visionary, resource allocator, relationship builder and crisis manager; keep sponsors social and strategic, never haggling over deliverables. Ask for a bounded commitment (a practitioner suggestion of roughly 10–12 hours a year plus a monthly touchpoint), brief them before each meeting (context, objective, attendees with GRASP notes, what not to discuss, desired next step) and plan sponsor succession. Take problems up with options: issue, impact, options with pros and cons, recommendation, decision deadline.

## 10. Account pod and team health

Pods of about 5–9 people covering commercial, delivery, finance and digital skills; a RASIC per phase (responsible, accountable, supporting, informing, contributing); a Belbin role mix check; team-contingent bonuses; a quarterly five-question pulse on Lencioni's dysfunctions (do we admit mistakes, debate strategy, commit to the plan, hold each other to account, prioritise the client's results?).

## 11. One-page key-account plan

1. Pragmatic strategy (five questions). 2. Account type and relationship position, now vs target. 3. GRASP map. 4. 9-box SWOT. 5. Value hypotheses with financial quantification. 6. Penetration and whitespace plan. 7. Joint KPIs including the customer's own measures. 8. Pod RASIC and sponsor role. 9. Risks and contingencies. 10. Relationship-quality score. 11. Contact and review cadence. 12. Decisions required from management.
