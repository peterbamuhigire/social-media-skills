# Reputation, Authority and Network System

**When to read:** when building a reputation plan for an executive, consultant, founder or professional; choosing how they will earn authority; designing their advisory board and network routine; or packaging a leadership-reputation retainer. Parent skill: [strategy-personal-brand](../SKILL.md). This is the engine's own method, synthesised from reputation and networking practice — including Brown, R. (2016) *Build Your Reputation*, Capstone/Wiley, Ivan Misner's Visibility–Credibility–Profitability sequence, Hewlett's sponsor research, Prahalad and Hamel on core competence, and Maltz et al. (1998) on introductions. No statistics from these sources are used.

| Input | Source | If missing |
|---|---|---|

## 1. Brief the client on why reputation matters

Explain in one page: reputation is what relevant people say when the person is not in the room. It earns **respect** (treated as a peer, defended, referred, not haggled with), **attention** (chosen in a crowded field) and **money** (fees, choice, autonomy) — sometimes remembered as REP (Brown). It cannot be fully controlled, grows slowly, differs from fame, will be challenged, is shaped by perception, is not the same as "no scandal", becomes public (screenshots travel on WhatsApp within minutes), can differ across work, community, faith and online settings (aim for consistency), and cannot be built while invisible. Never promise a financial return.

**Quick diagnosis:** is the person waiting to be noticed, busy without a plan, planning without acting, or planning and acting? Watch for two traps even in capable people: excellent but unknown, and well known but ordinary.

## 2. Audit (entry diagnostic, 2 weeks)

1. **Search footprint:** first three pages of results, images and news; share the person controls.
2. **Stakeholder words:** 6–10 people describe the person in three to five words plus one improvement. Where politeness norms mute criticism of seniors (common in East Africa), use an anonymous form or a third-party interviewer and indirect questions ("What do people usually ask you about her?").
3. **Perception gap:** the person rates themselves 1–10 on about 30 observable behaviours (writing, presenting, punctuality, handling setbacks, relating to seniors, peers and juniors) and predicts others' ratings; compare with step 2.
4. **What cannot be copied:** separate what every competitor offers from what only this person has — values, story, method, relationships, published ideas (Brown calls this the "red box").
5. **Competitive scoring:** list what the audience values; score the person and up to three rivals out of 10; find open ground.
6. **Capability test:** for each claimed strength, score relevance, difficulty to imitate and breadth of application (0–3 each).
7. Optional: a personal SWOT and psychometric results.

**Deliverables:** stakeholder word cloud, perception-gap chart, a one-line statement of what cannot be copied, three points of difference, a 12-month plan.

## 3. Plan across four levers

Pick two or three tactics per lever that match the person's head start, what they want to be known for, and their temperament; review each quarter.

| Lever | Tactic bank |
|---|---|
| Delivery (credibility) | Keep small promises visibly; deliver early; prepare meetings and circulate notes; add small unexpected service touches; ask for feedback; apologise quickly |
| Visibility | Local-media expert source; a short report or book used as a calling card; boards, panels and committees; research interviews with senior people; guest lectures; association roles; events followed by a written summary for sponsors; publicly recommending others |
| Positioning | One authority route (section 4); a short personal manifesto; points of difference versus points of parity; a category-of-one line; origin story; elevator pitches for different audiences; prepared answers for hard questions; testimonials captured at thank-you moments |
| Advocates | Advisory board with a sponsor (section 5); contact cadence; value-for-value exchanges |

Sequence effort as visibility → credibility → profitability (Misner's VCP Process): do not ask for referrals or fees before people have seen the work and trust it.

## 4. Choose one authority route

| Route family | Routes | Local example |
|---|---|---|
| Original thinking | Challenge an orthodoxy; improve an old solution; turn a field into a named framework; combine two ideas | A named five-step export-readiness method for Ugandan coffee co-operatives |
| Convening | Broker information; make deals; organise events; connect people; host a niche community | A curated WhatsApp community for Nairobi hospital CFOs |
| Reporting | Research and publish; review; interpret the news; surface a hidden fact | An annual consent-based survey of Kampala SMEs' digital spending, published with a chamber |
| Craft | Mastery of a tool or craft; a cause; authorship; early adoption of a new channel | A radio-and-LinkedIn column on agribusiness finance |

Route sequence: a real contribution → content → first advocates → the platform where the audience already is → an audience that talks among itself → regular thought leadership. Test every core idea with DCPR — distinctive, consistent, *passonable* (can someone repeat it accurately after hearing it once?), relevant (Brown). The research route is under-used where data is scarce; run surveys with a partner association, with consent and in line with the applicable data-protection law (Uganda DPPA 2019, Kenya DPA 2019).

## 5. Advisory board and sponsor

**Selection checklist:** mixed levels, sectors and temperaments; values before titles; people who have helped before and people who should matter more; together they cover the person's gaps; separate business and personal boards; an odd number (5 or 7); people the person knows and trusts; aligned with the development plan; refreshed over time; something offered in return.

**Invitation (compliment → purpose → light commitment → give-back):** "[Name], may I ask you something? I've long admired how you [specific]. I'm forming a small board of advisors for the next stage of my career. Would you consider a seat? In practice it's a 20-minute call every couple of months, or lunch once a quarter, on me. And I'd like to be useful to you too — for example [offer]." Keep a list of about 20 candidates; treat a "no" as "not now".

**Running it:** one-to-one conversations rather than full meetings; send an agenda; report back what happened; give something back each quarter; thank people specifically; pay the bill; respect their time.

**Sponsor versus mentor:** a mentor advises; a sponsor spends their own standing to open doors (Hewlett). Honour a sponsor with performance. **Guardrail:** where an advisor or sponsor is a public official, check conflict-of-interest, gifts and anti-corruption rules before any hospitality or introduction.

## 6. Network routine

1. **Map the network** into four groups: operational (today's work), professional (associations, alumni, peers), strategic (people shaping the future of the field) and personal (family, faith, community, trusted trades). The professional and strategic groups need scheduled effort.
2. **Find gaps by role:** who guides, who challenges, who plans with them, who has specialist knowledge, who encourages, who sponsors, who connects, who champions them publicly, who collaborates, who they model themselves on — and which of these roles the person plays for others.
3. **Score new connections** on usefulness, credibility, reach, goodwill, and what the person can do for them.
4. **Match the ask to the help needed:** information, practical advice, political or strategic support, or encouragement.
5. **Learn their agenda:** ask about their pains, problems, projects and plans before asking for anything.
6. **Set a cadence:** A contacts weekly to monthly, B quarterly, C once or twice a year, D reactive only; prune gently.
7. **Build fast for a season** (for a job move or launch): accept more connections and ask for introductions for a set period, with a follow-up routine so the effort is not wasted.
8. **Keep people informed:** "If you hear of anything in [area], please don't keep me a secret."

## 7. Leadership-reputation retainer (90 days)

| Weeks | Work |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Audit (section 2) |
| 3 | Positioning: authority route, points of difference, origin story, elevator pitches |
| 4 | Network design: map, gap audit, board shortlist, contact cadence in a CRM |
| 5–12 | One flagship asset; weekly posts from a topic bank; one senior research interview a month; one speaking or panel slot; two media pitches; public recommendations; events with written summaries; board recruitment |
| 13 | Review: visibility (reach, invitations), credibility (testimonials, citations, speaking invitations), profitability (referrals, inbound opportunities, fee level) |

A leader's personal reputation and the organisation's are linked; pair every retainer with message discipline and spokesperson preparation ([playbook-crisis-communications](../../../playbooks/playbook-crisis-communications/SKILL.md), [playbook-pr-media-integration](../../../playbooks/playbook-pr-media-integration/SKILL.md)).

## 8. Language checks

- Remove filler: "basically", "literally", "kind of", "I think", "you know".
- Accept thanks without erasing the effort: "Thank you — I was glad to make it happen", not "It was nothing".
- Share results when asked, as short specific stories.
- Point of difference: "Like others, we [parity item]. What is different is [point of difference], which means you [benefit]."
- Rewrite example (original): "I sort of do digital things for companies." → "I help Mbarara dairy co-operatives sell direct to Kampala supermarkets; this season two of them signed supply agreements." (verify the result before use)
