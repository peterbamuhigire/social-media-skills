# Digital Transparency Framework

Merged from skills/frameworks/framework-digital-transparency on 2026-09-29 at ce3299a; preservation map: [framework-digital-transparency.md](../../../../docs/kaizen/consolidation-2026-09-29/preservation/framework-digital-transparency.md)

## When to use this reference

Read this reference when the client's problem is consumer trust in its digital presence rather than a CSR programme: buyers hesitate to pay online, ask "how much?" in DMs, abandon carts, doubt reviews or worry about their data. It gives a transparency audit across three dimensions (clarity, openness, objectivity), calibration by generation, the Raw Marketing rules for authentic communication, the data-protection baseline and a Digital Transparency Report. The parent [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md) owns purpose and CSR claims, the CSR audit and impact storytelling; apply the same evidence-first rule here: audit before recommending, and show rather than claim.

Apply the framework only to a named, observed trust or accountability gap, and record the intervention chosen. Drafting recommendations does not authorise changing live accounts, collecting data or publishing.

Evidence status. The regression coefficients and model come from Rageh (2026); the percentages attributed to Denny and Leinberger (2020) and the Generation Z figure have no register record: verify before stating them to a client. Data-protection points are pointers, not legal advice; check the register records named in § Regulatory context and confirm with counsel.

## Why transparency matters in East Africa

In Uganda and East Africa many consumers are buying online for the first time, or have met fraud, misrepresentation or undelivered orders from online sellers. Their scepticism is not irrational; it is lived experience.

Rageh (2026) reports the chain:

> **Transparency → Trust (β = 0.730) → Commitment (β = 0.525) → Online Engagement (β = 0.413)**

The source argues that brands investing in visible, operational transparency see higher conversion and retention than brands that treat trust as implicit ("people will just see that we're legitimate"); cite the client's own before-and-after data rather than a general uplift. Transparency has to be designed into every touchpoint; it does not come from good intentions.

## Decision rules

| Situation | Action | Failure avoided |
|---|---|---|
| The observed behaviour and trust gap fit this framework | Apply it to the named behaviour and record the intervention chosen | Applying an abstract framework without a real trust or accountability problem |
| No audit has been done | Run the three-dimension audit and record gaps before any recommendation | Assuming transparency from the brand's self-presentation |
| Audience spans several generations | Use the practices of the most sceptical generation as the baseline | A uniform approach that underperforms with key segments |
| A negative review appears | Respond publicly within 24 hours using the six-step protocol | Silence read as indifference; hidden complaints read as manipulation |
| A values or social position is proposed | Check it is backed by visible operational evidence and unites rather than divides the audience | Brand-militancy backlash; values performed for attention |
| Paid amplification or data collection is planned | Fix clarity gaps on owned channels and resolve data-protection gaps first | Paying to send traffic to an unclear channel; unlawful processing |

## Procedure

### 1. Transparency audit

Audit before recommending; record each gap specifically.

**Clarity**

- [ ] Can a new visitor answer all five pre-purchase questions (below) from the website alone?
- [ ] Is pricing published on all owned platforms, or is "message for price" the default?
- [ ] Is the refund and returns policy visible without hunting for a footer link?
- [ ] Does the About page or bio show real names and photographs of the people behind the brand?

**Openness**

- [ ] Is there an active process for asking for reviews after purchase?
- [ ] Are reviews shown publicly on at least two platforms?
- [ ] Is there a clearly communicated feedback channel (email, WhatsApp or form)?
- [ ] Do all marketing lists use opt-in consent?

**Objectivity**

- [ ] Are negative reviews visible and publicly answered?
- [ ] Do testimonials and case studies include an honest account of challenges or obstacles?
- [ ] Is the aggregate rating shown, not only selected positive quotes?

### 2. Apply the three-dimensional model (Rageh, 2026)

**Clarity** — complete, accurate, easy-to-understand information on products, pricing, policies and the brand. A buyer must be able to answer five questions without contacting the business:

1. What exactly is this product or service?
2. What does it cost, including all fees and delivery?
3. How and when will I receive it?
4. What happens if something goes wrong?
5. Who is behind this brand — real people I can hold accountable?

A buyer who cannot answer all five without a DM tends to abandon the purchase; each unanswered question is a lost sale.

| Channel | Clarity action |
|---|---|
| Website | Full pricing (or "starting from UGX X" with clear scope); return and refund policy; delivery timeframes; About page with real names, photos and roles |
| Social media bio | Answer, or link to answers for, all five questions; never make a DM the only route to basic pricing |
| WhatsApp Business | Greeting message offering immediate self-service options; catalogue kept at current prices (see [platform-whatsapp](../../../platforms/platform-whatsapp/SKILL.md)) |
| Email | Physical address, business registration number where applicable, and an unsubscribe link in every footer |

**Openness** — interactive feedback and permission-based data collection, so customers feel heard and in control of their data.

- **Ask for reviews actively** on Google Business Profile, Facebook and sector platforms, in every post-delivery or post-service message. Never filter, hide or selectively display reviews (review programmes: [strategy-ewom-reviews](../../strategy-ewom-reviews/SKILL.md)).
- **Visible feedback channels** — a dedicated WhatsApp number or chat option for complaints, a review link in every post-purchase message, and a monthly Q&A on Instagram Stories or Facebook Live (Uganda: confirm Facebook access at the campaign date; register UG-FACEBOOK-ACCESS-2026).
- **Permission-based data collection** — never add a contact to a WhatsApp broadcast or email list without explicit, informed consent; state what is collected, how it is stored and how to opt out (WHATSAPP-BUSINESS-POLICY; § Regulatory context).
- **Respond in public** — thank compliments publicly; acknowledge complaints publicly and offer to resolve them privately. Silence signals indifference.

**Objectivity** — show negative information alongside positive. Counter-intuitively, brands that show 3-star reviews next to 5-star ones are trusted more; Rageh (2026) reports that consumers read a perfect record as manipulation or curation, not quality.

| Element | Conventional practice | Objectivity standard |
|---|---|---|
| Review display | Only 4- and 5-star reviews | Aggregate rating plus a sample across all ratings |
| Testimonials | Most enthusiastic praise | Include one honest challenge the client faced alongside the result |
| Case studies | Only successes | A section on what was difficult and how it was resolved |
| Product descriptions | Every benefit | Honest limitations, including who the product is not for |
| Negative reviews | Ignore or hide | Public response within 24 hours with a specific resolution or acknowledgement |

Negative-review protocol:

1. Respond within 24 hours, publicly, on the platform where the review appeared.
2. Thank the reviewer.
3. Acknowledge the specific issue without deflecting or blaming the customer.
4. Offer a specific resolution, not a generic apology.
5. Move the detail to a private channel (WhatsApp or direct message).
6. Follow up once resolved to confirm satisfaction.

### 3. Calibrate by generation (Rageh, 2026)

One approach applied to every generation underperforms. Where the audience spans several, use the most sceptical generation's practices as the baseline. Age bands and drivers are general; check against the client's own audience data (see [03-audience-personas](../../../pipeline/03-audience-personas/SKILL.md)).

| Generation | Trust drivers | Transparency calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Generation Z (born 1997–2012) | Brand activism; authentic values; peer reviews; creator endorsement | Source states 73% expect brands to take positions on social and environmental issues (verify before stating); highly alert to performative transparency, so back every stated value with visible action |
| Millennials (born 1981–1996) | Data control; co-creation; openness on data use | Explain data use explicitly; invite input into product and content; admit mistakes openly |
| Generation X (born 1965–1980) | Institutional credibility; credentials; endorsements from recognised authorities | Show certifications, memberships, awards and media coverage; name staff and their qualifications |
| Baby Boomers (born 1946–1964) | Personal service; traditional business ethics; conventional trust markers | Physical address, landline, named staff; written policies and guarantees; formal tone |

### 4. Apply the Raw Marketing rules (Denny and Leinberger, 2020)

Rageh treats transparency as a structural quality; Denny and Leinberger add a behavioural one, Raw Marketing: unscripted, unfiltered, human-first communication driven by a shift in consumer power. They report that 67% of purchase decisions were driven by consumer-initiated pull rather than brand push (2019 data; verify before stating). Use the five rules as filters on every content decision:

| Rule | Meaning | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Seeking Control | Consumers want control over how they meet and interact with brands | Offer choice: long and short form, video and text, read and listen; forced formats feel invasive |
| Unscripted | Audiences detect and reject produced, PR-managed content | Real staff voices, real customer stories, real behind-the-scenes footage; a slightly imperfect video from a real person beats a glossy one from a faceless brand |
| In-Process | People prefer to see work in progress, not only results | Document the journey: the product being made, the problem being solved, the team at work; "here is what we are working on" beats "here is what we achieved" |
| In-Context | Authentic content sits in the brand's and customers' real environment | Film in real workplaces, not studios; real customers in real settings, not models; real local conditions rather than aspirational imagery |
| Heroic Credibility | The highest trust comes from demonstrable, costly, values-aligned action | See below |

**Heroic Credibility** is trust earned when a brand takes a position that costs it revenue, customers, convenience or approval; it cannot be manufactured. Examples: publishing prices when competitors hide them; openly stating product limitations; an article recommending competitors for some use cases; turning away a customer who is not the right fit; an environmental or social commitment that affects profit. In East Africa, a business that publishes what competitors charge, says when it is not the right choice and shows real job sites and staff builds this faster than advertising can.

**Brand-militancy failures.** Political or social positions that divide rather than unite the audience can carry severe commercial cost. Test: is this a value the brand holds and lives, or a position adopted for attention? Lived values build loyalty; performed values bring backlash. Before any values communication, confirm visible operational evidence, not just a post (the parent skill's journalist test applies).

**The C2B shift.** Denny and Leinberger (2020) argue consumer-to-business has replaced business-to-consumer as the dominant dynamic: buyers research independently, self-select, set the agenda for sales conversations and share experiences publicly whatever the brand prefers. Design for a buyer who is already most of the way through the decision before contact (the source says 70%; verify before stating).

### 5. Regulatory context

Uganda's Data Protection and Privacy Act 2019 and Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019 align with this framework:

- **Consent** — collect and process personal data only with informed, freely given, specific consent.
- **Data minimisation** — collect only what the stated purpose needs.
- **Breach notification** — notify the relevant authority and affected people of a breach within the period the law sets (confirm the current period with counsel).
- **Direct-marketing opt-out (Uganda)** — honour a written request to stop processing for direct marketing (UG-DPPA-S26-DIRECT-MARKETING-2026).

Register records: UG-DPPA-2019, UG-DPPR-2021, UG-PDPO-ORG (registration with the Personal Data Protection Office), UG-DPPA-S26-DIRECT-MARKETING-2026, KE-DP-GENERAL-2021, KE-ODPC-DIRECT-MARKETING-2026. For Tanzania or Rwanda, check the register's national records first. Not legal advice.

Treating transparency only as compliance undersells it: present it to the client as both a revenue driver and a legal baseline.

## Output: Digital Transparency Report

1. **Audit results** — completed checklists for all three dimensions, with gaps identified.
2. **Clarity recommendations** — actions per channel, each with a responsible person and a deadline.
3. **Openness recommendations** — mechanisms to configure, including review requests and the feedback channel.
4. **Objectivity recommendations** — the standard applied to current testimonials, case studies and review display.
5. **Generational calibration note** — which generation dominates the audience and how practices are weighted.
6. **Regulatory compliance checklist** — actions to align with the applicable data-protection law.
7. **Review schedule** — repeat the audit every six months; trust practices decay unless maintained.

## Release checklist

1. All three dimensions are audited before any recommendation; gaps are documented, not assumed.
2. Clarity fixes are in place on all owned channels before paid amplification starts.
3. Openness mechanisms are configured and tested: review requests running, feedback channel live, opt-in records kept.
4. Objectivity is applied: negative reviews visible and answered publicly; at least one testimonial or case study includes an honest point of friction.
5. Practices are calibrated to the audience's generational profile, not applied uniformly.
6. Data-protection gaps are flagged and resolved before any personal data is collected or processed for marketing.
7. The audit is scheduled to repeat every six months.
8. Every percentage or coefficient shown to the client is attributed to its source, and unregistered figures are verified or removed.
