# Community Trust Framework (Like-Know-Trust)

## When to use this reference

Read this reference when a community-management engagement needs a trust diagnosis and trust-sequenced content before (or alongside) day-to-day moderation: a new brand with no proof, a business recovering from a reputation issue, an organisation entering a competitive market, or a page with high reach and engagement but few enquiries. It gives the Like-Know-Trust (LKT) arc, a stage diagnostic, content by stage, the 10-4-1 mix, five trust-acceleration tactics, stage metrics and the extended trust model (transparency dimensions, commitment, influencer types, generational calibration). The parent [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md) owns response SLAs, templates, escalation and the monthly health scorecard.

Apply the framework only to a named, observed behaviour and trust gap, and record the intervention chosen; an abstract framework applied without a real trust or accountability problem is the failure to avoid. Structural transparency (disclosure, data and policy openness) is owned by `strategy/strategy-csr-purpose-communications`; this reference covers trust sequencing inside the community relationship.

Evidence status. The LKT arc, 10-4-1 ratio, content weightings and review targets are practitioner heuristics from the cited books, not measured East African benchmarks. The Rageh (2026) path coefficients are reported study results; state them as such and verify before quoting to a client (no register record).

## The Like-Know-Trust arc

LKT describes the sequence a stranger travels before becoming a paying customer. It is a relationship arc, not a funnel: skipping stages does not accelerate conversion, it destroys it. The model maps closely to the social media buying cycle in Bodnar and Cohen (2012), in which value is given before a sale is ever attempted.

- **Like.** Before an audience pays attention, they must find the brand worth following. Content is entertaining, practically useful or emotionally resonant, and does not sell. A brand that opens with "buy from us" is asking for trust it has not earned. In East Africa, where word of mouth and community reputation carry great commercial weight, a brand that is not liked is not shared, and a brand that is not shared does not grow organically. Like-stage content creates the conditions for everything that follows.
- **Know.** Once the audience likes the brand, they want to understand it: who the business is, what it stands for, how it works, who is behind it and what it believes. Behind-the-scenes content, founder stories, values posts and FAQ content move audiences through this stage. Its commercial consequence is reduced price sensitivity: audiences who know a brand are buying a relationship, not just a product. The effect is especially pronounced in Uganda and the wider East African market, where consumers routinely pay a premium for businesses they personally trust.
- **Trust.** The most commercially consequential stage. Trust is demonstrated, not declared, through consistency, proof and risk reduction: social proof (reviews, testimonials, case studies), third-party endorsements (press coverage, certifications, awards), guarantees and transparency. Only when trust is established does selling become natural and welcome rather than intrusive. Where online fraud and quality inconsistency are live consumer concerns, as across East Africa, the Trust stage is the gate every conversion must pass.

Extended arc (see "Extended trust model" below): **Like → Know → Trust → Commitment → Advocacy**.

## Decision rules

| Situation | Action | Failure avoided |
|---|---|---|
| New or existing client, no strategy yet | Run the stage diagnostic before recommending any content | Content that skips the audience's actual stage |
| High reach and engagement, low conversion | Treat as stuck at Know; add Trust-stage content | Pushing offers at an audience without enough proof to act |
| Strong conversion, poor organic growth | Treat as bypassing Like; add Like-stage content | Selling only to existing customers and recruiting no new audience |
| Calendar has no Like / Know / Trust content | Add the missing stage: no Like = not growing; no Know = not deepening; no Trust = not converting | Unbalanced calendar |
| Client over-posts promotions | Adopt the 10-4-1 mix and explain the shift | Audiences ignoring promotional-heavy pages |
| New brand, reputation recovery, or competitive entry | Apply the five trust-acceleration tactics in sequence | Slow trust build while competitors convert |
| Trust metrics plateau despite strong Like and Know | Re-run the diagnostic and introduce one or more acceleration tactics | Guessing at the conversion gap |
| Like metrics static | Audit for drift toward promotional-heavy posting; rebalance with 10-4-1 | Stalled audience growth |
| Audience spans several generations | Add the generational calibration layer | One trust approach misfiring across cohorts |

## Procedure

### 1. Diagnose the current stage

Score the audience on each signal. Where the strongest signals cluster is the current dominant stage; where signals are absent are the gaps to close.

| Audience signal | Like | Know | Trust |
|---|---|---|---|
| Follows or engages with content passively | ✓ | | |
| Tags friends in posts | ✓ | | |
| Shares content to their own feed or stories | ✓ ✓ | | |
| Comments and asks questions | | ✓ | |
| Searches for the brand by name | | ✓ | |
| Sends a direct message or WhatsApp enquiry | | | ✓ |
| Submits a quote request or booking form | | | ✓ |
| Purchases without additional reassurance | | | ✓ ✓ |
| Recommends to friends unprompted | | | ✓ ✓ ✓ |
| Complains openly and expects resolution | | | ✓ (high expectation = high trust) |

Interpretation: high reach and engagement with low conversion usually means stuck at Know (aware and interested, not yet enough proof to act). Strong conversion with poor organic growth usually means Like-stage content is being bypassed. Record a clear verdict on the dominant stage and the gaps.

### 2. Map content to stages

Map every piece of content to one LKT stage before publishing.

**Like content**
- Entertainment: humorous posts, relatable everyday situations, feel-good stories relevant to the target audience.
- Inspiration: success stories and aspirational content reflecting the audience's own ambitions.
- Practical value: tips and how-tos that help immediately, with no purchase required and no strings attached.
- Aesthetic: product photography, appealing food shots, aspirational lifestyle imagery.
- East Africa examples: a Kampala bakery sharing a short morning video of fresh bread being made, with the aroma described in the caption; a law firm posting "5 common legal mistakes Uganda business owners make (and how to avoid them)".

**Know content**
- Founder and team stories: who started the business, why, and what drives them daily.
- Behind the scenes: how the product is made or the service delivered; the team at work.
- Values posts: what the brand believes, how it treats customers and staff, what it will and will not do.
- FAQ content: the most common customer questions answered openly, honestly and without evasion.
- East Africa examples: a private clinic in Entebbe sharing a video of the lead doctor explaining their patient-first philosophy; a Ugandan manufacturer showing the production floor and quality-check process.

**Trust content**
- Customer testimonials: WhatsApp message screenshots, video reviews, written narratives with the customer's name and face where consented.
- Case studies: before-and-after narratives and problem-solved stories with specific outcomes (for example "we reduced their legal costs by 40%" — use only the client's verified figures).
- Certifications and affiliations: for example Uganda Medical Association membership, ISO certification, Uganda Revenue Authority TIN displayed publicly.
- Media coverage: "As featured in Daily Monitor", "Mentioned in NTV Uganda Business Report" (only where true).
- Guarantees: "If you are not satisfied, we will refund — no questions asked" (only where the client operates it).
- Transparent complaint resolution: publicly resolving a customer issue in the comments shows confidence and integrity more powerfully than advertising.
- East Africa examples: a financial services firm sharing anonymised client outcome data ("Client saved UGX 12 million in tax in year one"); a secondary school sharing UNEB results and university placement rates.

### 3. Apply the 10-4-1 mix

Use the 10-4-1 rule (Bodnar and Cohen, 2012) as the practical LKT content mix for any active channel:

- **10 shares** of third-party content (Like and Know): relevant articles, sector news and useful resources from credible external sources; builds authority and perceived generosity without selling.
- **4 original posts** (Know and Trust): brand-produced educational content, behind-the-scenes footage, team stories, FAQ answers and case studies.
- **1 promotional post** (Trust, leading to conversion): a direct offer, service announcement, event invitation or call to action.

Most East African clients currently over-post promotional content, so 10-4-1 needs a structural shift in how they think about social media. Frame it clearly: audiences in Uganda are becoming more sophisticated and less responsive to promotional content (practitioner observation; no register record); they follow brands for value, not advertisements. The ratio creates the trust that makes the single promotional post welcome rather than ignored.

Deliverable: a sample 15-post sequence using 10-4-1, mapped explicitly to the client's current LKT stage and target conversion goal.

### 4. Accelerate trust where needed

For new brands, businesses recovering from a reputation issue, or organisations entering a competitive market, apply these five tactics in sequence:

1. **Rapid review generation.** Deploy a WhatsApp review request template within the first 30 days. Target: 10 genuine, named Google reviews in the first month. Use the review request protocol in [playbook-reputation-management](../../playbook-reputation-management/SKILL.md). Never condition service or incentives on a positive review.
2. **Proof-first content weighting.** For the first 90 days weight the calendar 60% Trust / 30% Know / 10% Like. This deliberately reverses the usual growth-first sequence to address an active trust deficit before broad audience development.
3. **Transparency post.** Publish one post that confronts the most common customer concern directly, for example: "The most common question we get is 'can I really trust an online business in Uganda?' Here is our answer." It disarms the objection before the customer raises it and positions the brand as confident rather than evasive.
4. **Social proof cluster.** In a single week publish five testimonials or case studies in quick succession. Clustering creates a perception of strong positive evidence that one monthly testimonial never achieves. Use only consented, genuine material.
5. **Founder visibility campaign.** Put the founder's face on content for 30 consecutive days: one short video, post or story per day. In East African markets people trust people before logos; a face creates accountability, relatability and connection that branded graphics cannot.

### 5. Measure stage progression

Track with platform-native metrics, report monthly, and use movement across the table to judge whether sequencing is working.

| Stage | Primary metric | Secondary metric | Target direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Like | Follower growth rate (%) | Reach per post | Increasing month on month |
| Know | Engagement rate (%) | Comments as a ratio of total interactions | Increasing; comments-to-likes ratio improving over time |
| Trust | Enquiry rate (DMs, WhatsApp, forms) | Conversion rate (enquiry → sale) | Increasing; conversion rate rising quarter on quarter |
| Advocacy | Unprompted tags and referral mentions | User-generated content (UGC) volume | Increasing; track through brand-mention monitoring |

When Trust metrics plateau despite strong Like and Know numbers, diagnose with step 1 and add tactics from step 4. When Like metrics are static, audit for promotional drift and rebalance with 10-4-1.

## Extended trust model

### Three-dimensional transparency (Rageh, 2026)

Apply within the Trust stage. Trust is built through three simultaneous dimensions, all of which must be active:

- **Clarity:** complete, easy-to-find information about the brand, its products, pricing and policies. Information gaps create suspicion.
- **Openness:** interactive feedback channels and permission-based data collection. Brands that ask before they take build more trust than brands that assume consent.
- **Objectivity:** negative reviews displayed alongside positive ones. Brands that show only five-star feedback are disbelieved; brands that respond to criticism publicly and professionally are trusted.

Reported empirical chain (Rageh, 2026): transparency → trust (β = 0.730) → commitment (β = 0.525) → engagement (β = 0.413). Transparency produces trust, trust produces commitment and commitment drives engagement, in that sequence rather than simultaneously. Report these as one study's coefficients, not as a client forecast.

### Commitment as the terminal stage

The goal of LKT is commitment building, not trust acquisition: commitment is reported as the strongest predictor of online engagement (Rageh, 2026). Committed members do not merely trust the brand; they defend it, recruit for it and return regardless of price fluctuation. At this stage: recognise committed members publicly, give them early access and exclusive involvement, and create participation structures (polls, co-creation, feedback panels) that give them a sense of ownership of the brand's direction.

### Four types of social influencer (Westergaard, 2016)

Map community amplifiers with this taxonomy before prescribing an engagement strategy.

| Type | Reach | Action probability | Investment priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Connected Catalysts | Broad | Low | Low — wide reach, shallow engagement |
| Passionate Publishers | Medium | High content output | Medium — content volume, variable quality |
| Everyday Advocates | Narrow | High | **High — the most underinvested type** |
| Altruistic Activators | Narrow | Maximum loyalty | **High — deepest brand attachment** |

Most community strategies over-invest in Catalysts (macro-influencers) and under-invest in Advocates and Activators (micro and nano advocates). A fan-elevation approach moves members up the engagement ladder, from passive follower to Advocate or Activator. Identify Everyday Advocates through unprompted brand mentions, peer referrals and comment quality, then activate them through direct recognition and exclusive involvement. Paid creator work belongs to `pipeline/08-influencer-marketing-strategy`.

### Generational trust calibration (Rageh, 2026)

Do not apply one trust-building approach across audiences spanning several age cohorts:

- **Gen Z (born 1997–2012):** demands brand activism and algorithmic transparency; will not engage with brands that appear performatively progressive without structural commitment.
- **Millennials (born 1981–1996):** prioritise data control and privacy; trust rises when brands give clear control over data and communication frequency.
- **Generation X (born 1965–1980):** responds to institutional credentials, certifications and third-party endorsements; professional standing matters more than peer social proof.
- **Baby Boomers (born 1946–1964):** value personal service and traditional authority signals: named contacts, direct phone access and relationship history.

When the audience spans several generations, add this as a segmentation layer in the persona work ([03-audience-personas](../../../pipeline/03-audience-personas/SKILL.md), which owns the full generational method).

## Release checklist

1. The three LKT stages are each defined in their own paragraph with the commercial logic in the East African market context.
2. The stage diagnostic is applied to the specific client and gives a clear verdict on the dominant stage and the gaps.
3. Content examples for every stage include at least one concrete East African or Ugandan scenario.
4. The 10-4-1 rule is cited (Bodnar and Cohen, 2012), mapped to LKT stages and used to produce a client-specific 15-post sequence.
5. Trust-acceleration tactics are numbered 1–5, each with a concrete action and timeframe.
6. Measurement gives a primary and secondary metric per stage with a stated direction of travel.
7. Bodnar and Cohen (2012) is cited at least twice: at the LKT introduction and at the 10-4-1 rule.
8. Every testimonial, figure, certification and media mention is the client's own verified material; reported study figures are labelled as such.
