# Agency Growth Roadmap

**When to read:** when an agency owner asks how to grow from where they are, which clients and niche to pursue, what to charge, when to hire, or why clients leave. Parent skill: [playbook-agency-operations](../SKILL.md). The method below is the engine's own, drawing on agency-building practice including Nelson, J. (2019) *The Seven Figure Agency Roadmap*, Seven Figure Agency LLC, and on Kelley & Sheehan's agency-management principles. All money figures are illustrative UGX scenarios; re-base them on the owner's real cost base and never present them as benchmarks.

## Inputs

| Input | Source | If missing |
|---|---|---|
| Current monthly recurring revenue (MRR), client count, average fee | Owner's invoices or accounts | Build the plan on a stated assumption and flag it |
| Monthly client churn over the last 6–12 months | Client list with start and end dates | Assume a conservative churn and label it |
| Close rate, meeting show rate, reply rate | CRM or diary | Run one month of tracking before committing to targets |
| Cost of delivering each service | Delivery team, supplier invoices | Complete the cost-to-serve sheet (section 5) first |
| Owner's time map | Owner | Run the role-map exercise (section 6) |

## 1. Decide the business model before the marketing

Answer four questions in writing before spending on lead generation: what exactly is sold (a named programme, not a menu of tools); to whom (one niche); at what monthly fee; and whether it recurs. An agency that markets an unclear model stays busy without growing. Plan growth on three fronts at once — winning clients, delivering results, keeping clients — and check each quarter that none is neglected.

## 2. Growth diagnosis and gap maths

**Diagnose the owner's current constraint by revenue band** (bands are illustrative UGX MRR; re-base locally):

| Band (illustrative) | Typical constraint | Priority move |
|---|---|---|
| Below UGX 10m | Owner does everything; no proof base | Win three proof clients in one niche |
| UGX 10–20m | Cannot yet afford a hire | Raise fee and fix payment discipline before adding clients |
| UGX 20–60m | Owner still in delivery | First operations hire; documented procedures |
| UGX 60–150m | Team leadership | Account management layer; margin control |
| Above | Systems and leadership | Second niche or partner channel only after the first is stable |

**Gap procedure:**
1. Record current MRR and the MRR wanted in 12 months; the difference is the gap.
2. Divide the gap by the planned fee to get net new clients; add expected churn to get gross wins.
3. Back-solve the pipeline: wins per month ÷ close rate = review meetings; ÷ show rate = bookings; ÷ reply rate = outreach volume or event registrants.
4. Choose lead sources that can realistically produce that number of bookings; "post more" is not a lead source.
5. Set monthly, quarterly and annual targets, each with a diary date to stop, review and adjust.

**Scenario (illustrative):** a Kampala agency bills UGX 18m a month (12 clients × UGX 1.5m) and wants UGX 60m in a year. At a new UGX 3m programme that is 14 net new clients, about 18 gross wins allowing for churn — 1.5 a month. At a 25% close rate that is 6 review meetings a month; at a 70% show rate, about 9 bookings. The plan must name the lead sources that will produce 9 bookings each month.

## 3. Choosing the fee level

1. Build a table of four to six fee levels for the annual revenue target: clients needed, gross wins per month after churn, meetings needed.
2. Note that selling a large programme usually takes about as much effort as selling a small one; fewer, larger clients are usually easier to serve well.
3. Treat the smallest-fee, many-client path as a warning sign for a small team (for example 100 clients at UGX 1m to reach UGX 100m MRR).
4. If the agency cannot command a meaningful monthly fee from firms that already spend on marketing, suspect that the problem solved is too small or the proof too thin — fix those before discounting.
5. An entry offer must be a paid diagnostic that leads into the core programme, never a cheap version of the programme; value-ladder thinking (Brunson) applies only where each rung genuinely serves the next.

## 4. Choosing a niche

Score candidate niches from 1 to 5 on each test and choose the highest total that the founder can serve credibly:

| Test | Question | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| Self-recognition | Would a member read a headline naming the group and think "this is for me"? | Try two headline wordings in a small ad or post |
| Spending habit | Does the group already pay for advertising or promotion? | Visible ads, radio, outdoor, directory and search activity |
| Enough firms | Are there enough firms of the right size within reach? | Registries, association rolls, directories; widen to the EAC or diaspora if one country is too small, or accept fewer, larger firms at a higher fee |
| Gathering point | Is there an association, event or community that reaches them? | Member lists, events, speaking slots (check current access and data-sharing rules) |
| Founder fit | Does the founder's background give credibility? | Past roles, languages, relationships |
| Transaction value | Is one extra customer worth many times the monthly fee? | Average sale value of the client's customers |

Prefer a specialty within a specialty (orthodontics within dentistry, boutique lodges within hospitality). Win two or three proof clients locally before widening. Commit to the chosen niche for 12 months before judging it.

## 5. Designing the programme and its economics

**Pair fast and slow results.** Every retainer needs one component that produces enquiries within weeks (paid search or social to a landing page, a WhatsApp enquiry flow) alongside the components that compound over months (search visibility, content, reputation). Clients who see nothing for months cancel before the slow work pays. Wrap a website build as the set-up phase of a recurring programme rather than a stand-alone project. Standardise page frameworks and batch content so delivery is repeatable without looking identical across clients. Check any search tactic (location pages, citations, link building) against the current Google Search spam policies before use.

**Cost-to-serve sheet.** One row per deliverable with columns: item · supplier or staff cost · basis (one-off set-up, monthly, yearly) · hours. Typical lines: design, content, hosting and software, on-page and reputation work, call tracking, reporting, welcome kit, payment-processing fees. Add East African lines: mobile-money merchant fees, withholding tax and VAT treatment (route to `chwezi-accounting-doctrine`), data bundles and power backup, courier, USD-priced software and exchange-rate risk, and senior hours. Price for a margin that funds growth; flag any client whose cost to serve breaks the margin floor.

**Presenting price.** Offer no more than three programmes. Present in this order: the problem → what it costs the client → how the programme solves it → the price. When size signals suggest the fee is out of reach, state the range early. Close with a choice between two options and book the proposal walk-through before sending anything. Details: [biz-dev-proposal](../../../business-development/biz-dev-proposal/SKILL.md) and [biz-dev-pricing-menu](../../../business-development/biz-dev-pricing-menu/SKILL.md).

## 6. Building the team

**Role-map exercise:**
1. Draw every function: marketing, sales, account management, operations, finance and administration, and each delivery line.
2. Write the owner's name in every box they currently fill.
3. Mark the boxes that drain the owner most (hire for these first) and the boxes filled badly (fix or replace).
4. Write role profiles for the next two hires; have someone who enjoys process document the procedure for each drained box.
5. Keep a light recruiting pipeline open even when not hiring; hire people who enjoy the work the owner finds a chore.
6. Plan the hand-off: owner sells and launches → owner sells, another person launches → owner is the public face, a business-development lead sells and an account manager runs clients.

Rule of thumb (heuristic, not a law): a solo owner hits a ceiling where adding clients damages delivery; when that happens, build capacity rather than pausing sales.

## 7. Onboarding and the monthly rhythm

**First two weeks of a new client:** a set-up sheet (access, assets, services, contacts, approval owner); a launch call that includes the writer, covering goals, what the client does better than anyone locally, other marketing and service priorities; a short welcome sequence from several team members over the first two to three months; a printed summary, a locally made gift and a handwritten note; a small surprise each quarter.

**Monthly review agenda:** relationship check → work done → results (trend, tracked leads or calls) → plan for the next 30/60/90 days → seasonal questions (school terms, festive season, Eid, harvest, tourism peaks) → two pieces of client homework → next call date. Open the following month by checking the homework. Log every attempt to reach a client who goes quiet. The three to four months after launch are the usual danger window — change gear and show early signals.

**Templates:**
- Launch call: "Before we build anything, help us understand who you most want more of, what you do better than anyone in [town], and what has worked before."
- Monthly close: "Here is what we did, what moved, and the next 90 days. Two things we need from you before our next call: [homework 1] and [homework 2]."
- Seeding the next quarter: "Looking ahead, is there a seasonal push, new service or event we should build around now?"

## 8. Failure patterns to check each quarter

Cheap one-off websites with no recurring programme; assuming clients will not pay a proper fee without testing firms that already advertise; a generalist client list; switching niche after two or three months; mixing projects and retainers without a plan; manual, late payment collection; cold outreach as the only lead source; contacting the list once a month; perfectionism that delays webinars or content; pitching on the first call; criticising a prospect's website; slide-by-slide sales meetings; sending proposals without a booked walk-through; slow-return-only programmes; automated reports with no conversation; assuming results alone keep clients; not measuring retention monthly.
